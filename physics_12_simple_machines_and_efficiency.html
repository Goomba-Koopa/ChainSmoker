Physics • Topic 12

Simple Machines & Mechanical Advantage

Definition and functions of a machine, Mechanical Advantage (\(MA\)), Velocity Ratio (\(VR\)), Efficiency (\(\eta = MA/VR\)), The Principle of Moments, Classification of Levers (Class I, II, III), and Pulley systems.

1. Core Quantities of Simple Machines

If you tried to pry open a tightly sealed paint can lid with your bare fingernails, you would fail and likely hurt your fingers. But take a sturdy metal screwdriver, slide the tip under the rim, and press gently on the handle: the lid instantly pops off with minimal effort. A screwdriver acts as a simple machine—a device that overcomes a large resistive force (Load) by applying a relatively smaller force (Effort) at a convenient point and in a desired direction.

Four Vital Functions of a Machine: 1. As a Force Multiplier: Overcoming a heavy load by applying a small effort (e.g. car jack, crowbar, bottle opener).
2. In Changing the Point of Application of Effort: Shifting effort to an accessible spot (e.g. bicycle pedals turning a rear wheel through a chain).
3. In Changing the Direction of Effort: Pulling downward with gravity to lift an object upward (e.g. a single fixed pulley lifting a bucket of water from a well).
4. For Gaining Speed: Moving the load through a large distance rapidly when a larger effort is exerted (e.g. a pair of scissors cutting cloth, or swinging a badminton racket).
Fundamental Formulas: Mechanical Advantage, Velocity Ratio & Efficiency
1. Mechanical Advantage (\(MA\)): The ratio of the resistive load (\(L\)) overcome to the effort (\(E\)) applied: $$MA = \frac{\text{Load }(L)}{\text{Effort }(E)}$$ • If \(MA > 1\): The machine acts as a force multiplier.
• If \(MA < 1\): The machine provides a gain in speed.
• If \(MA = 1\): Used solely to change the direction of force.

2. Velocity Ratio (\(VR\)): The ratio of the displacement of effort (\(d_E\)) to the displacement of load (\(d_L\)) in the same time interval: $$VR = \frac{\text{Displacement of Effort }(d_E)}{\text{Displacement of Load }(d_L)}$$ Key Axiom: \(VR\) is a pure geometric property dependent solely on machine dimensions; it never changes even when friction is present.

3. Work Efficiency (\(\eta\)): The ratio of useful work output to total work input, expressed as a percentage: $$\eta = \frac{\text{Work Output}}{\text{Work Input}} = \frac{L \times d_L}{E \times d_E} = \left(\frac{L}{E}\right) \times \left(\frac{d_L}{d_E}\right) = \frac{MA}{VR}$$ $$\therefore \mathbf{MA = \eta \times VR} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{\eta = \frac{MA}{VR} \times 100\%}$$
[†] Thermodynamic Limitation: The 100% Efficiency Myth For an ideal machine without friction or weight in its moving parts, Work Output = Work Input, meaning \(\eta = 100\%\) and \(MA = VR\). However, in every real-world machine, a portion of the input energy is inevitably converted into thermal energy due to friction at bearings and the mechanical weight of the machine parts. Therefore, for all real machines, \(\mathbf{\eta < 100\%}\) and \(\mathbf{MA < VR}\).

2. The Principle of Moments & The Three Classes of Levers

A lever is a rigid bar capable of turning freely about a fixed pivot point called a Fulcrum (\(F\)). Under the Principle of Moments in rotational equilibrium: $$\text{Load} \times \text{Load Arm} = \text{Effort} \times \text{Effort Arm} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \mathbf{MA = \frac{\text{Effort Arm}}{\text{Load Arm}}}$$

Class of Lever Central Pivot Order Mechanical Advantage (\(MA\)) Everyday Real-World Examples
Class I Lever Fulcrum (\(F\)) in the middle (between Load and Effort).
Order: \(L - F - E\)		 Can be \(>1\), \(=1\), or \(<1\) depending on whether Effort arm is longer, equal, or shorter than Load arm. • Crowbar, See-saw, Wire-cutting pliers (\(MA > 1\)).
• Physical beam balance (\(MA = 1\)).
• Tailor's shears cutting cloth (\(MA < 1\), gain in speed).
Class II Lever Load (\(L\)) in the middle (between Fulcrum and Effort).
Order: \(F - L - E\)		 Always \(MA > 1\) because the Effort arm (\(FE\)) is strictly longer than the Load arm (\(FL\)). Always acts as a force multiplier. • Nutcracker cracking a hard walnut shell.
• Wheelbarrow carrying heavy bricks.
• Soda bottle cap opener prying a metal crown cap.
Class III Lever Effort (\(E\)) in the middle (between Fulcrum and Load).
Order: \(F - E - L\)		 Always \(MA < 1\) because the Effort arm (\(FE\)) is strictly shorter than the Load arm (\(FL\)). Sacrifices force for a gain in speed & distance. • Sugar tongs or ice tongs picking up cubes.
• Human forearm lifting a weight in hand.
• A broom sweeping across a large floor.

3. Pulley Systems: Fixed vs. Movable Systems

A pulley is a grooved wooden or metallic wheel that rotates around an axle passing through its center, around which a rope passes.

Pulley Type Mechanical Advantage (\(MA\)) Velocity Ratio (\(VR\)) Primary Function & Mechanism
Single Fixed Pulley \(MA \approx 1\) (\(MA < 1\) with friction) \(VR = 1\) Does not multiply force. Its sole purpose is to change the direction of effort from an awkward upward pull to a convenient downward pull where the operator can use their own body weight.
Single Movable Pulley \(MA \approx 2\) (\(MA < 2\) with friction) \(VR = 2\) Acts as a true force multiplier. The load is shared between two supporting rope segments, requiring only half the effort (\(E \approx L/2\)) to lift the weight.
Block and Tackle System (with \(n\) pulleys) \(MA = n\) (ideal) \(VR = n\) (strictly equals total number of pulleys \(n\)) Combines multiple fixed and movable pulleys. Used in construction cranes and shipyards to lift several metric tons of steel with a small electric motor.

4. Worked Problems

Problem 12.1: Lever Equilibrium & Effort Calculation
A rigid uniform crowbar of total length \(150\text{ cm}\) is pivoted at a fulcrum located \(30\text{ cm}\) from a heavy boulder of mass \(60\text{ kgf}\).
1. Identify the class of this lever.
2. Calculate the minimum effort force required at the opposite end of the crowbar to lift the boulder.
Step-by-Step Logical Resolution:

1. Classification:
• The fulcrum is positioned between the boulder (Load) and the operator's hands (Effort).
• This is a Class I Lever (\(L - F - E\)).

2. Effort Calculation:
• Load Arm = \(30\text{ cm}\).
• Effort Arm = Total Length \(-\) Load Arm \(= 150\text{ cm} - 30\text{ cm} = \mathbf{120\text{ cm}}\).
• Applying the Principle of Moments:
$$\text{Load} \times \text{Load Arm} = \text{Effort} \times \text{Effort Arm}$$ $$60\text{ kgf} \times 30\text{ cm} = E \times 120\text{ cm}$$ $$E = \frac{1800}{120} = \mathbf{15\text{ kgf}}$$ Physical Deduction: Because the effort arm is \(4\times\) longer than the load arm (\(MA = 4\)), the operator needs to exert only \(15\text{ kgf}\) of effort to lift the \(60\text{ kgf}\) boulder.
Problem 12.2: Block and Tackle System Efficiency
A block and tackle system contains a total of \(5\text{ pulleys}\). An effort of \(125\text{ N}\) is applied to lift a load of \(500\text{ N}\).
Calculate: (i) Mechanical Advantage (\(MA\)), (ii) Velocity Ratio (\(VR\)), and (iii) The percentage efficiency (\(\eta\)) of the pulley system.
Step-by-Step Logical Resolution:

1. Mechanical Advantage:
$$MA = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{Effort}} = \frac{500\text{ N}}{125\text{ N}} = \mathbf{4.0}$$
2. Velocity Ratio:
• For a block and tackle system, \(VR\) is strictly equal to the total number of pulleys in both blocks.
$$VR = n = \mathbf{5}$$
3. Efficiency (\(\eta\)):
$$\eta = \frac{MA}{VR} \times 100\% = \frac{4.0}{5.0} \times 100\% = \mathbf{80\%}$$ Physical Deduction: The system is \(80\%\) efficient. The remaining \(20\%\) of input energy is dissipated overcoming friction in the 5 rotating pulley bearings and lifting the weight of the lower movable pulley block.

5. Give Scientific Reasons

Machine Dynamics 1: Single Fixed Pulley Mechanical Advantage
Why is a single fixed pulley used to draw water from a well even though its mechanical advantage is slightly less than 1 (\(MA \le 1\))?
Scientific Resolution:
A single fixed pulley does not multiply force, but it changes the direction of applied effort to a much more convenient downward direction (where the worker can utilize their own body weight). Pulling downwards is physically far easier and safer than pulling a heavy bucket vertically upwards.
Machine Dynamics 2: Machine Efficiency Under 100%
Why can the efficiency (\(\eta\)) of any real practical machine never be exactly \(100\%\)?
Scientific Resolution:
In every real mechanical system, a portion of input energy is inevitably wasted doing work against friction between moving components and lifting the weight of the machine parts themselves (e.g., pulley ropes and blocks). This dissipated energy converts into heat and sound, making \(\text{Work Output} < \text{Work Input}\) and \(\eta < 100\%\).
