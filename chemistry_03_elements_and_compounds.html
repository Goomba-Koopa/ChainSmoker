Chemistry • Topic 03

Elements, Compounds & Mixtures

Definitions of element, compound, and mixture; the Law of Constant Proportions; the five-point Compounds vs Mixtures matrix; and separation techniques (filtration, distillation, chromatography, magnetic separation).

1. Elements: The Fundamental Building Blocks

Think of a piece of pure 24-carat gold jewellery, a solid iron nail, or the helium gas filling a bright party balloon. If you were to zoom into any of these materials down to the absolute smallest possible microscopic scale, you would find that every single particle is identical. These substances cannot be broken down into any simpler ingredients because they are essentially the universe's most basic chemical building blocks. Every element has unique properties and a specific symbol on the Periodic Table.

Element: An element is a pure substance consisting entirely of one type of atom. It cannot be broken down or decomposed into two or more simpler substances by any ordinary chemical or physical means (such as heating, cooling, or electrolysis).

2. Compounds & The Law of Constant Proportions

When you drink a glass of water, you are actually consuming a chemical compound. Water is formed when hydrogen (which is a highly explosive gas) and oxygen (a gas that actively supports burning) chemically bond together in a very specific way. Interestingly, the newly formed compound—water—is a liquid that extinguishes fire, completely losing the dangerous properties of its parent elements. Furthermore, whether you collect that water from a kitchen tap, a flowing river, or a melting glacier in the Arctic, every single water molecule will always have exactly two hydrogen atoms for every one oxygen atom by mass.

Compound: A compound is a pure substance formed by the chemical combination of two or more distinct elements in a fixed ratio by mass. The physical and chemical properties of a compound are entirely different from the properties of its constituent elements.
Law of Constant Proportions (Proust's Law): This fundamental chemical law states that a pure chemical compound always contains the exact same elements chemically combined together in the exact same fixed proportion by mass, regardless of its source, origin, or method of preparation.

3. Mixtures & The 5-Point Distinction Matrix

Think of a large bowl of fruit salad or the air you breathe every day. In a fruit salad, you can easily separate and pick out the grapes from the apples because they are simply physically mixed together; they are not chemically bonded into a new "grape-apple" fruit. Similarly, the air around us is a mixture of nitrogen, oxygen, argon, and carbon dioxide. Each gas within the air retains its own individual chemical properties, and the overall composition of air can vary slightly depending on where you are—such as in a busy city versus a dense forest.

Mixture: A mixture is an impure substance consisting of two or more pure substances (elements or compounds) physically mixed together in any arbitrary proportion, without any chemical bonding taking place between them.
Characteristic Compounds Mixtures
Composition Constituents are always present in a strict, fixed ratio by mass. Constituents can be present in any arbitrary or variable ratio.
Properties Properties are entirely different from those of its original constituents. The mixture retains the original properties of all its individual constituents.
Separation Constituents can only be separated by complex chemical methods (e.g., electrolysis). Constituents can be easily separated by simple physical methods (e.g., filtration, distillation).
Energy Change Formation is accompanied by a significant absorption or release of energy (heat/light). Formation involves no significant energy change or release.
Melting/Boiling Point Has a sharp, definite melting and boiling point due to its pure nature. Does not have a definite melting or boiling point (it varies with the mixture's exact composition).

4. Separation Techniques for Mixtures

Because the components of a mixture are not chemically bonded together, their individual identities remain intact. We can exploit differences in their physical properties—such as particle size, boiling point, density, solubility, or magnetic attraction—to cleanly separate them without chemical reactions.

Deep Dive: Distillation (Separation by Boiling Point)
1. Principle: Distillation relies on the substantial difference in the boiling points of the components in a homogeneous liquid mixture. It combines two sequential physical phase changes: $$\text{Liquid} \xrightarrow{\text{Boiling / Vaporization}} \text{Vapour} \xrightarrow{\text{Cooling / Condensation}} \text{Pure Liquid (Distillate)}$$ 2. Simple Distillation: Used to separate a soluble solid from a liquid solvent (e.g. recovering pure water from saline sea water), or two miscible liquids whose boiling points differ by more than \(25^\circ\text{C}\). The mixture is heated in a round-bottom distillation flask. The lower-boiling liquid vaporizes first, travels through a water-cooled Liebig Condenser, and condenses into a receiving flask as pure distillate, leaving the non-volatile solid residue behind in the flask.

3. Fractional Distillation: Used when two or more miscible liquids have boiling points that are very close to one another (difference \(< 25^\circ\text{C}\)), such as a mixture of Ethanol (b.p. \(78^\circ\text{C}\)) and Water (b.p. \(100^\circ\text{C}\)), or in the industrial refining of crude petroleum. A fractionating column packed with glass beads is inserted between the flask and condenser to provide repeated condensation-vaporization cycles, ensuring only the most volatile component exits the top.
Deep Dive: Centrifugation (Separation by Density under High Speed)
1. Principle: When tiny solid particles suspended in a liquid are too small to settle rapidly by gravity alone (or would pass right through filter paper), centrifugation is used. The liquid mixture is placed in centrifuge tubes and spun at thousands of revolutions per minute in a centrifuge machine.

2. Mechanism: The rapid rotation generates a powerful outward centrifugal force that is many times stronger than Earth's gravity. The denser solid particles are forced outward and pack tightly at the bottom of the tube (forming a solid pellet), while the lighter liquid floats on top as a clear supernatant layer that can easily be decanted.

3. Real-World Applications: • Dairy Industry: Separating lighter cream/fat droplets from denser skimmed milk.
• Clinical Diagnostics: Rapidly spinning blood samples in medical pathology laboratories to separate heavy red and white blood cells from liquid blood plasma.
• Washing Machines: The spin-dry cycle uses centrifugal force to fling water droplets out through the perforated drum walls.
Separation Technique Governing Physical Principle Representative ICSE Application
Filtration Difference in particle size between insoluble solid and liquid solvent. Separating suspended chalk powder or insoluble sand from water.
Distillation Difference in boiling points of volatile liquid components. Obtaining pure distilled water from mineral/salt solutions.
Centrifugation Difference in density accelerated by high-speed rotational force. Separating cellular components from blood plasma in diagnostic labs.
Sublimation One component transitions directly from solid to gas on heating without melting. Separating Ammonium Chloride (\(\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}\)) or Iodine from Salt.
Separating Funnel Difference in densities of immiscible liquids that form distinct layers. Separating an oil and water mixture by draining the denser lower layer.
Magnetic Separation Selective attraction of ferromagnetic substances by a magnetic field. Extracting iron filings from powdered sulphur or magnetic ore from sand.
Chromatography Differences in solubility and absorption rates across a stationary paper medium. Resolving compound dyes in black fountain pen ink into separate colours.

5. Worked Problems

Problem 3.1: Mass Proportions in Compounds [3 Marks]
A sample of water contains 2 g of hydrogen and 16 g of oxygen. A second sample has 4 g of hydrogen. How much oxygen does the second sample contain? State the law used.
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. Identify the ratio in the first sample:
    Mass of Hydrogen = \(2\text{ g}\)
    Mass of Oxygen = \(16\text{ g}\)
    Ratio by mass (H : O) = \(2 : 16 = \mathbf{1 : 8}\) [1 Mark]

2. Apply the ratio to the second sample:
    Mass of Hydrogen = \(4\text{ g}\)
    Let the mass of Oxygen be \(x\text{ g}\).
    According to the established ratio: \(\frac{1}{8} = \frac{4}{x}\)
    Therefore, cross-multiplying gives: \(x = 4 \times 8 = \mathbf{32\text{ g}}\) of Oxygen. [1 Mark]

3. State the relevant scientific law:
    This calculation is based on the Law of Constant Proportions, which states that a chemical compound always contains its component elements in a fixed ratio by mass, regardless of the sample size. [1 Mark]
Problem 3.2: Sequential Mixture Separation [4 Marks]
A student has a mixture of iron filings, salt, and sand. Describe step-by-step how you would separate each component. Name the technique used at each step.
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. Step 1: Isolate the Iron Filings
    • Technique: Magnetic Separation
    • Method: Hover a strong bar magnet over the dry mixture. The magnetic iron filings will be attracted to the magnet and removed completely, leaving behind only the non-magnetic salt and sand. [1.5 Marks]

2. Step 2: Dissolve the Salt
    • Technique: Dissolution
    • Method: Add plenty of water to the remaining sand and salt mixture, and stir thoroughly. The soluble salt dissolves entirely into the water to form a salt solution, while the insoluble sand remains solid. [0.5 Mark]

3. Step 3: Isolate the Sand
    • Technique: Filtration
    • Method: Pour the aqueous mixture through filter paper placed in a funnel. The insoluble sand (residue) is trapped on the filter paper, while the clear salt solution (filtrate) passes through into a collecting beaker below. [1 Mark]

4. Step 4: Isolate the Salt
    • Technique: Evaporation (or Distillation)
    • Method: Gently heat the filtrate in an evaporating dish until all the water evaporates. Solid salt crystals will be left behind in the dish. [1 Mark]

6. Give Scientific Reasons

Q: Why is water classified as a compound and not a mixture?
Reason: Water (\(\text{H}_2\text{O}\)) is formed by a chemical reaction between hydrogen and oxygen in a fixed mass ratio of 1:8, adhering to the Law of Constant Proportions. Furthermore, its properties are completely different from its constituents—hydrogen is highly combustible and oxygen supports combustion, whereas water extinguishes fire. Its components cannot be separated by simple physical means, which is a defining characteristic of a compound.

Q: Why do iron filings and sulfur powder form a mixture when simply stirred together, but iron sulfide when heated strongly?
Reason: Stirring them together simply intermingles the particles without any chemical bonds forming, meaning they retain their original properties (e.g., iron can still be magnetically separated and sulfur can be dissolved in carbon disulfide). Heating provides the activation energy necessary for a chemical reaction to occur, permanently bonding the iron and sulfur atoms together to form iron sulfide (a compound) with entirely new chemical and physical properties.

6. Give Scientific Reasons

Chemical Classification 1: Water as a Compound
Why is water (\(\text{H}_2\text{O}\)) classified as a chemical compound and not as a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen gases?
Scientific Resolution:
1. Hydrogen and oxygen combine in a fixed mass ratio of \(1:8\) during a chemical reaction involving energy release.
2. Water possesses completely unique properties (it is a liquid that extinguishes fire), whereas hydrogen is highly inflammable and oxygen supports combustion. The constituents cannot be separated by simple physical methods.
Chemical Classification 2: Air as a Mixture
State TWO scientific reasons why atmospheric air is considered a mixture and not a compound.
Scientific Resolution:
1. The constituent gases in air (nitrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide, argon) retain their individual chemical identities without forming new chemical bonds.
2. The proportions of air components vary by altitude and location, and constituents can be separated by fractional distillation of liquid air.
