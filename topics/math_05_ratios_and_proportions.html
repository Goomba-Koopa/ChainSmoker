The Grade 6 Bridge

From Grade 6 Intuition to Grade 7 Reality

Mathematics builds layer upon layer. Here is how Grade 6 recipe comparisons evolve into Grade 7 formal proportions:
Grade 6: The Simple Model Visual Recipe Scaling & Part Sharing
  • Intuitive Recipe Ratios: Ratios were seen as simple cooking proportions (2 cups water to 1 cup rice) or sharing sweets.
  • Identical Given Units: Quantities were almost always presented in identical units; converting paise to rupees was rarely tested inside ratios.
  • Assumed Direct Variation: All scaling was assumed to be direct: if 2 pens cost Rs 10, 4 pens cost Rs 20 (inverse work-time was not introduced).
Grade 7: The Deeper Reality Formal Proportion Theory & Dual-Mode Variation
  • Dimensionless Purity: A ratio \(a : b\) compares magnitudes of identical dimensions; units cancel completely, making the ratio a pure number.
  • Equivalence Axiom: Four terms form a proportion \(a : b :: c : d \iff ad = bc\); three terms form a continued proportion \(\iff b^2 = ac\).
  • Bifurcated Unitary Method: Problems strictly distinguish Direct Variation (\(\frac{y}{x} = \text{const}\)) from Inverse Variation (\(x \cdot y = \text{const}\), e.g. workers \(\times\) days).

1. The Anatomy of a Ratio: Comparative Scaling

Imagine you are making the ultimate mango milkshake. The secret family recipe requires 2 cups of mango pulp for every 3 cups of chilled milk. If you want to make enough for a birthday party of thirty friends, how much pulp and milk do you buy?

You do not care about the absolute total volume at first; you care about the relationship between pulp and milk. That relationship is a ratio: an invariant comparison of two quantities of the same kind, obtained by dividing the first quantity by the second.

Ratio A numerical comparison of the magnitude of two quantities \(a\) and \(b\) of the exact same kind and unit, written as: $$\frac{a}{b} \quad \text{or} \quad a : b$$ The first term \(a\) is termed the antecedent, and the second term \(b\) is termed the consequent.
The Three Golden Laws of Ratios

1. Dimensionless Purity: Because a ratio divides one measurement by another of the identical unit (e.g., \(40\text{ cm} / 100\text{ cm}\)), the units cancel completely. A ratio is a pure scalar number with no unit attached. Writing "\(2 : 3\text{ kg}\)" is an absolute mathematical error.

2. Order Matters Irrevocably: The ratio \(a : b\) is fundamentally distinct from \(b : a\). In our milkshake, \(2 : 3\) gives sweet nectar; \(3 : 2\) gives thick baby puree.

3. Lowest Terms Reduction: Multiplying or dividing both antecedent and consequent by the same non-zero scalar \(k\) preserves the ratio: $$a : b = ka : kb = \frac{a}{k} : \frac{b}{k}$$ Always reduce ratios to coprime integers by dividing by their Highest Common Factor (HCF).

Heads-Up • Unit Consistency in Ratios

Always convert both quantities into identical units before calculating a ratio. For example, comparing \(50\text{ paise}\) to \(\text{Rs } 2\):
Convert rupees to paise first: \(\text{Rs } 2 = 200\text{ paise}\).
Simplified ratio: \(\frac{50}{200} = \frac{1}{4} = 1 : 4\).

2. Partitioning Quantities: The Total Parts Method

A classic• ICSE problem tasks you with dividing a prize pool, a chemical solution, or a land perimeter in a specified ratio \(a : b : c\). How do we partition a concrete total \(T\)?

The "Common Part" Variable Derivation
Let two people share a total quantity \(T\) in the ratio \(a : b\).
Because \(a : b\) is the simplified fraction, there exists an unknown scaling multiplier \(x\) (the size of one single "share" or "slice") such that: $$\text{First Share} = ax, \quad \text{Second Share} = bx$$
The sum of the individual shares must balance the total pool \(T\): $$ax + bx = T \implies x(a + b) = T \implies x = \frac{T}{a + b}$$
Substituting \(x\) back into each share produces the universal partitioning formulas: $$\text{First Share} = a \left( \frac{T}{a + b} \right) = \frac{a}{\text{Sum of Terms}} \times T$$ $$\text{Second Share} = b \left( \frac{T}{a + b} \right) = \frac{b}{\text{Sum of Terms}} \times T$$

Always perform the Consistency Check: Add your calculated shares together. If their sum does not strictly equal the original total \(T\), an arithmetic error occurred.

3. Proportions & The Balance of Equivalences

What happens when two separate ratios are identical in value? That equality forms a proportion.

Proportion An equality of two ratios. If \(a : b = c : d\), we say the four numbers \(a, b, c, d\) are in proportion, denoted: $$a : b :: c : d \quad \iff \quad \frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}$$ The outer terms \(a\) and \(d\) are the extremes; the inner terms \(b\) and \(c\) are the means.
Fundamental Theorem of Proportion
Multiplying both sides of \(\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}\) by the product of denominators \(b \cdot d\): $$\frac{a}{b} \cdot (bd) = \frac{c}{d} \cdot (bd) \implies a \cdot d = b \cdot c$$ $$\mathbf{\text{Product of Extremes} = \text{Product of Means}}$$
This golden cross-multiplication identity allows us to instantly solve for any missing fourth term when three terms are given.

Continued Proportion & The Mean Proportional

Three quantities \(a, b, c\) are in continued proportion if the ratio of the first to the second equals the second to the third:

$$\frac{a}{b} = \frac{b}{c} \implies b^2 = ac \implies b = \sqrt{ac}$$

Here, \(b\) is termed the mean proportional between \(a\) and \(c\), while \(c\) is the third proportional to \(a\) and \(b\).

4. The Unitary Method: Direct vs. Inverse Variation

The Unitary Method is the universal Swiss army knife of commercial arithmetic: find the value of a single unit first, then scale that single unit up to find the target total.

Feature Direct Variation (Constant Quotient) Inverse Variation (Constant Product)
Core Law As quantity \(x\) increases, quantity \(y\) increases in exact proportion. As quantity \(x\) increases, quantity \(y\) decreases such that their product remains fixed.
Mathematical Invariant $$\frac{y}{x} = k \quad (\text{Constant Rate})$$ $$x \cdot y = k \quad (\text{Constant Total Work / Pool})$$
Real-World Examples • More petrol consumed = greater distance travelled.
• More notebooks purchased = higher total price. 		• More workers deployed = fewer days to finish a building.
• Faster vehicle speed = less travel time for a fixed journey.
Action for Unit (1) DIVIDE total cost by total units to get unit cost. MULTIPLY current workers by days to get total "man-days".
Action for Target MULTIPLY unit cost by requested quantity. DIVIDE total pool by the new number of workers.
Heads-Up • Inverse Variation in Work Problems

If 6 workers complete a task in 10 days, how long will 12 workers take?
Common Misconception: Treating work time as direct variation.
Correct Inverse Formulation: Total work = \(6 \times 10 = 60\text{ worker-days}\).
For 12 workers: \(\text{Days} = \frac{60}{12} = 5\text{ days}\).

5. Worked Problem• ICSE Examination Problems

Problem 5.1 • Dividing in Ratios The Three-Way Inheritance Distribution
A financial estate worth Rs 1,80,000 is divided among three siblings (Aarav, Bhavna, and Chirag) in the ratio \(2 : 3 : 5\). Calculate the exact monetary share received by each sibling.
Step 1: Determine the total parts.
$$\text{Sum of ratio terms} = 2 + 3 + 5 = 10\text{ parts}$$
Step 2: Calculate value per single part.
$$\text{Value of 1 part} = \frac{Rs\; 1,80,000}{10} = Rs\; 18,000$$
Step 3: Scale each share by its respective parts.
Aarav's share: \(2 \times Rs\; 18,000 = Rs\; 36,000\)
Bhavna's share: \(3 \times Rs\; 18,000 = Rs\; 54,000\)
Chirag's share: \(5 \times Rs\; 18,000 = Rs\; 90,000\)
Verification: \(Rs\; 36,000 + Rs\; 54,000 + Rs\; 90,000 = Rs\; 1,80,000\). Perfect balance.
Problem 5.2 • Continued Proportion Finding the Mean Proportional
Find the mean proportional between 8 and 18.
Step 1: Set up the proportion relation.
Let \(x\) be the mean proportional between 8 and 18.
By definition of continued proportion: $$\frac{8}{x} = \frac{x}{18}$$
Step 2: Cross-multiply.
$$x^2 = 8 \times 18$$ $$x^2 = 144$$
Step 3: Extract the square root.
$$x = \sqrt{144} = 12$$
Final Answer: The mean proportional is 12 (Notice: \(8/12 = 2/3\) and \(12/18 = 2/3\)).
Problem 5.3 • Inverse Unitary Method Hostel Food Provisions Allocation
A residential boarding school has food supplies sufficient for 120 students for 40 days. If 30 new students join the hostel on day 1, how many days will the provisions last?
Step 1: Identify variation type and compute total food capacity.
More students mean the food runs out faster. This is inverse variation.
$$\text{Total Provision Units} = 120\text{ students} \times 40\text{ days} = 4,800\text{ student-days}$$
Step 2: Find the updated population.
$$\text{New Total Students} = 120 + 30 = 150\text{ students}$$
Step 3: Calculate days of sustenance.
$$\text{Number of Days} = \frac{4,800\text{ student-days}}{150\text{ students}} = 32\text{ days}$$
Final Answer: The food will last for exactly 32 days.

6. Scientific & Mathematical Reasoning

Curiosity 5.1: Why Do Ratios Discard Absolute Reality?
If Team Alpha and Team Beta both have a win-to-loss ratio of \(3 : 1\), does that mean both teams won the exact same number of matches?
No, absolutely not. A ratio describes relative proportion, not absolute count.

Team Alpha might have played only 4 games (3 wins, 1 loss), while Team Beta might have played 40 games (30 wins, 10 losses). Both simplify to \(\frac{3}{1}\). A ratio reveals the quality of efficiency or distribution while completely hiding the scale of the system until an absolute scalar total is provided.
Curiosity 5.2: Can a Ratio Have Negative Terms?
In Class 7 geometry and physical measurements, why are negative numbers forbidden inside ratios?
In standard Class 7 arithmetic, ratios measure physical magnitudes—lengths, volumes, masses, counts of people, and monetary currency.

Because physical magnitudes cannot be negative (\(-3\text{ kg}\) of flour does not exist in nature), geometric and commercial ratios are strictly positive real numbers.
Exam Strategy • The 5-Second Filter

8. The Common-Sense Strategy: Reality Checks

Elimination Drill
"Common sense is the law." In ratios and proportions, physical relationships dictate numerical bounds. When more workers join a task, time must decrease; when scaling quantities, units must match. Before calculating complex fractions, test options against physical intuition and conservation laws.
Check 01 The Variation Direction Test

Is the relationship direct or inverse? In inverse variation (workers vs. time, speed vs. time), increasing one variable must decrease the other. Any option where time increases when workforce grows is immediately absurd.

Check 02 The Unit Homogeneity Rule

Ratios compare quantities in identical units. A ratio between centimeters and meters or hours and minutes without a metric conversion factor of 100 or 60 is an automatic red flag.

Check 03 Product Conservation (\(x \cdot y = k\))

In inverse variation, the total effort (worker-days) remains invariant. Multiply the candidate answer by the new condition; if the product does not equal the initial total work, eliminate it instantly.

Part (i): Mean Proportional [1 Mark]

Let the mean proportional between \(8\) and \(18\) be \(b\).
By definition of continuous proportion:
\[\frac{8}{b} = \frac{b}{18} \implies b^2 = 8 \times 18 = 144\] Taking positive square root:
\[b = \sqrt{144} = 12\]

Part (ii): Ratio in Simplest Form [2 Marks]

First convert both quantities to the same metric unit:
\(1\text{ m} = 100\text{ cm} \implies 1.5\text{ m} = 1.5 \times 100 = 150\text{ cm}\).
Now form the ratio:
\[\frac{45\text{ cm}}{150\text{ cm}} = \frac{45 \div 15}{150 \div 15} = \frac{3}{10}\] Expressed in ratio notation: \(3 : 10\).

Chief Examiner's Tip: Never compute a ratio until both terms share the exact same unit. In word problems involving hours and minutes, kilograms and grams, or meters and centimeters, always convert to the smaller unit first to eliminate decimals!
