Water as a Solvent, Solutions & Solubility

Solute, solvent, and solution definitions; Saturated vs Unsaturated vs Supersaturated solutions; solubility formula (S = mass of solute / 100g solvent); hard water vs soft water; temporary vs permanent hardness; and anomalous expansion of water.

From Grade 6 Intuition to Grade 7 Reality

Chemistry builds layer upon layer. Here is how what you learned in Grade 6 evolves into Grade 7 solubility rigor:
Grade 6: The Simple Model Everyday dissolving and qualitative saturation
  • Simple Mixing: Water dissolves sugar and salt until no more dissolves and solid settles at the bottom.
  • Thermal Speed: Heating water makes sugar dissolve faster and in slightly larger amounts.
  • Basic Components: A solution is just a solid mixed into a liquid medium.

  • Phase Equilibrium: Saturated solution is a dynamic equilibrium at fixed temperature; cooling yields crystallization.
  • Quantitative Formula: Solubility is strictly grams of solute per \(100\text{ g}\) of pure solvent (\(S = \frac{m_{\text{solute}}}{m_{\text{solvent}}} \times 100\)).
  • Gas De-solvation: Gas solubility decreases with temperature; heating water releases dissolved air bubbles well below \(100^\circ\text{C}\).

1. The Anatomy of a Solution

When you stir a spoonful of sugar into a hot cup of tea, the sugar granules seem to vanish entirely into the liquid. This disappearing act isn't magic; it is a physical process where the solid substance breaks down into its individual molecules and disperses uniformly throughout the liquid. This uniform mixture is a perfect example of a solution. Water, due to its ability to dissolve a vast array of substances, is widely known as a "universal solvent," though it specifically dissolves polar and ionic substances best.

Solution Components: • Solute: The substance that dissolves in a medium to form a solution (e.g., sugar, salt). It is usually present in a smaller proportion.
• Solvent: The medium in which the solute dissolves (e.g., water, alcohol). It is usually present in a larger proportion.
• Solution: A homogeneous mixture of two or more substances, formed when a solute completely dissolves in a solvent.
Term State Role in Mixture Everyday Example
Solute Can be solid, liquid, or gas The substance being dissolved Salt (solid) in saltwater, or Carbon dioxide (gas) in soda
Solvent Usually a liquid The substance doing the dissolving Water in saltwater, or Alcohol in perfumes
Solution Typically matches the state of the solvent The final homogeneous mixture Ocean water (salt + water), or Air (gas + gas)

2. Saturation and Solution Types

Imagine making a thick sugar syrup for a traditional sweet like gulab jamun. At first, the sugar dissolves easily in the water. But as you add more and more sugar, eventually it simply settles at the bottom of the pan, refusing to dissolve no matter how much you stir. This happens because the water has reached its maximum capacity to hold the sugar molecules at that specific temperature. If you heat the water, you can suddenly trick it into holding even more sugar.

Levels of Saturation: • Unsaturated Solution: A solution in which more solute can be dissolved at a given temperature without any settling at the bottom.
• Saturated Solution: A solution that cannot dissolve any more solute at a given temperature. Any additional solute will remain undissolved at the bottom.
• Supersaturated Solution: A highly unstable solution containing more dissolved solute than a saturated solution at a given temperature. It is created by saturating a solution at a high temperature and cooling it slowly.

3. Solubility and Its Calculation

Just like recipes require exact measurements, chemists need to know the precise amount of a substance that can dissolve in water. Some substances, like table salt, dissolve very easily in large amounts, whereas others, like baking soda, have a much lower limit. Solubility gives us a mathematical way to compare the dissolving power of different substances by standardized testing: measuring how many grams of the solute can completely dissolve in exactly 100 grams of the solvent.

Solubility Definition: The solubility of a solute in a particular solvent is defined as the maximum mass (in grams) of the solute that can be dissolved in \(100\text{ g}\) of the solvent at a specified temperature to form a saturated solution.
The Solubility Formula
To calculate solubility (\(S\)), we determine the ratio of the mass of the solute to the mass of the solvent, and scale it to \(100\text{ g}\) of solvent.

$$S = \left( \frac{\text{Mass of solute (in g)}}{\text{Mass of solvent (in g)}} \right) \times 100$$
Note: The mass of the solvent can be found by subtracting the mass of the solute from the mass of the total solution (if given). Because solubility is a ratio of masses multiplied by 100, it is usually expressed simply in grams per 100 g of solvent.

4. Hard Water vs. Soft Water

Have you ever tried washing your hands with soap from a well or a specific tap, only to find that instead of a rich, bubbly foam, a sticky, white scum forms on your skin? This occurs because the water in that area contains dissolved minerals picked up from underground rocks. When water travels through limestone or gypsum, it dissolves calcium and magnesium salts. These dissolved metal ions chemically react with soap, destroying its ability to lather.

Water Hardness Definitions: • Soft Water: Water that readily gives a rich lather with ordinary soap. It is essentially free from dissolved calcium and magnesium salts.
• Hard Water: Water that does not lather easily with soap, but instead forms a sticky, insoluble precipitate called "scum". It contains dissolved salts (bicarbonates, chlorides, or sulphates) of calcium and magnesium.

• Temporary Hardness: Hardness caused by the presence of dissolved bicarbonates of calcium and magnesium. It can be easily removed by simply boiling the water.
• Permanent Hardness: Hardness caused by the presence of dissolved chlorides and sulphates of calcium and magnesium. It cannot be removed by boiling, requiring chemical treatment (like adding washing soda).
Feature Temporary Hardness Permanent Hardness
Causing Salts Calcium bicarbonate \(Ca(HCO_3)_2\)
Magnesium bicarbonate \(Mg(HCO_3)_2\)		 Calcium/Magnesium Chlorides \((CaCl_2, MgCl_2)\)
Calcium/Magnesium Sulphates \((CaSO_4, MgSO_4)\)
Removal Method Can be removed by straightforward boiling. Cannot be removed by boiling; requires washing soda.
Boiling Reaction Soluble bicarbonate turns into insoluble carbonate (scale), which precipitates out. No reaction occurs upon boiling; ions remain dissolved.
Real-World Issue Forms chalky white scale inside kettles and hot water pipes. Interferes with industrial boilers and dyeing processes.

5. The Anomalous Expansion of Water

If you accidentally leave a sealed glass bottle completely filled with water in the freezer overnight, you will likely wake up to find the glass shattered. Almost all liquids in the universe shrink and become denser as they freeze into a solid. Water, bizarrely, breaks this rule. Below \(4^\circ\text{C}\), as it approaches its freezing point, water actually expands and becomes less dense. This strange behavior is the reason why ice cubes float in your drink, and why frozen lakes have liquid water underneath, allowing fish to survive the winter.

Anomalous Expansion: The unusual behavior of water whereby it contracts when heated from \(0^\circ\text{C}\) to \(4^\circ\text{C}\), and expands when cooled from \(4^\circ\text{C}\) to \(0^\circ\text{C}\). Consequently, water has its maximum density at exactly \(4^\circ\text{C}\).

6. Worked Problems

Problem 8.1: Solubility Calculation [3 Marks]
\(36\text{ g}\) of salt dissolves in \(200\text{ g}\) of water at \(20^\circ\text{C}\) to form a saturated solution. Calculate the solubility of the salt at \(20^\circ\text{C}\).
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. Identify Given Values:
    Mass of solute (salt) = \(36\text{ g}\)
    Mass of solvent (water) = \(200\text{ g}\) [1 Mark]

2. Apply the Solubility Formula:
    $$\text{Solubility } (S) = \frac{\text{Mass of solute}}{\text{Mass of solvent}} \times 100$$ [1 Mark]

3. Calculate:
    $$S = \frac{36}{200} \times 100 = \frac{36}{2} = \mathbf{18\text{ g / 100 g of water}}$$ [1 Mark]

Conclusion: The solubility of the salt at \(20^\circ\text{C}\) is \(18\text{ g}\) per \(100\text{ g}\) of water.
Problem 8.2: Hard Water Distinction & Treatment [4 Marks]
A student boils a sample of hard water for 10 minutes. Afterwards, the boiled water lathers freely with soap but the original sample did not.
(i) What type of hardness did the water have?
(ii) Write the chemical equation showing how boiling removes this hardness.
(iii) Name one salt that causes permanent hardness.
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. Part (i): Type of Hardness
    Since the hardness was removed simply by boiling (allowing it to lather with soap), the water had Temporary Hardness. [1 Mark]

2. Part (ii): Chemical Equation
    When temporary hard water is boiled, the soluble bicarbonates decompose into insoluble carbonates, which precipitate out.
    $$Ca(HCO_3)_2 \xrightarrow{\text{heat}} CaCO_3 \downarrow + H_2O + CO_2 \uparrow$$ [2 Marks]

3. Part (iii): Cause of Permanent Hardness
    Permanent hardness is caused by chlorides or sulphates. Example: Calcium sulphate (\(CaSO_4\)) or Magnesium chloride (\(MgCl_2\)). [1 Mark]
Problem 8.3: Give Scientific Reasons [2 Marks]
Give a scientific reason: Why do water pipes in extremely cold countries often burst during severe winters?
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

Due to the anomalous expansion of water, when the temperature outside drops below \(4^\circ\text{C}\), the water inside the pipes begins to expand as it cools towards \(0^\circ\text{C}\) and turns into ice. [1 Mark]
Since ice occupies a larger volume than liquid water, it exerts immense outward pressure on the inner walls of the rigid metal or plastic pipes, ultimately causing them to crack and burst. [1 Mark]

7. Give Scientific Reasons

Aquatic Chemistry 1: Aquatic Life in Frozen Lakes
Why do fish and aquatic animals survive at the bottom of frozen lakes in extreme winter climates?
Scientific Resolution:
Due to the anomalous expansion of water, water reaches its maximum density at \(4^\circ\text{C}\). As surface water cools below \(4^\circ\text{C}\), it becomes lighter and remains on top, eventually freezing into an insulating surface ice sheet at \(0^\circ\text{C}\), while dense liquid water at \(4^\circ\text{C}\) stays insulated underneath at the lake bed.
Aquatic Chemistry 2: Water as Universal Solvent
Why is water universally designated as the 'Universal Solvent'?
Scientific Resolution:
Water has a highly polar molecule with a high dielectric constant, allowing it to dissolve a wider variety of ionic salts, acids, bases, and polar organic substances than any other liquid known in nature.

8. The Common-Sense Strategy: Reality Checks

Elimination Drill
"Common sense is the law." In chemistry, formulas describe physical phenomena. Top problem solvers don't blindly grind algebra; they inspect options first and ask: Which of these are physically absurd or mathematically impossible? In solubility and solution problems, you can eliminate wrong choices in seconds by verifying solvent boundaries and gas de-solvation rules.
Check 01 Solvent Mass Isolation Rule

Solubility is strictly defined per \(100\text{ g}\) of pure solvent, NOT \(100\text{ g}\) of solution. Remember: \(m_{\text{solution}} = m_{\text{solute}} + m_{\text{solvent}}\). Denominators with total solution mass produce mass percentages, not solubility!

Check 02 Thermal Degassing Law

Unlike solids whose solubility generally increases with heat, the solubility of gases in liquids decreases as temperature rises. Heating drives dissolved atmospheric gases out well below \(100^\circ\text{C}\).

Check 03 Crystallization Difference Bracket

When cooling a hot saturated solution, the mass crystallized equals the difference between solubilities at the two temperatures: \(\Delta m = S_{\text{hot}} - S_{\text{cold}}\). Precipitated mass cannot exceed original dissolved mass!

B Steam / water vapor bubbles forming because the liquid has reached its boiling point.

⚡ Ruled Out in 2 Seconds Boiling Point Scale Violation
Why common sense eliminates this: Pure water boils at \(100^\circ\text{C}\) under normal atmospheric pressure. At \(32^\circ\text{C}\), the liquid is nowhere near its boiling point, so vapor bubbles cannot form throughout the bulk liquid.
C Carbon dioxide gas generated rapidly by microscopic bacterial respiration.
Click to test with common sense ⚡
⚡ Ruled Out in 5 Seconds Biological Speculation Trap
Why common sense eliminates this: Tap water is treated and sanitized. Microscopic bacteria do not produce visible effervescence clinging across glass walls within minutes of gentle temperature rise.
D Dissolved atmospheric air (oxygen and nitrogen) coming out of solution because gas solubility decreases as temperature rises.
Click to test with common sense ⚡
✓ The Only Survivor! Gas De-Solvation Confirmed
Why common sense picks this without calculating: Cold water dissolves substantial quantities of atmospheric gases (\(\text{O}_2, \text{N}_2\)). When warmed from \(10^\circ\text{C}\) to \(32^\circ\text{C}\), gas solubility drops sharply, expelling the excess gas as tiny clinging air bubbles.

The 10-Second Physical Proof:
• Gas dissolution in water is exothermic: \(\text{Gas} + \text{Water} \rightleftharpoons \text{Aqueous Solution} + \text{Heat}\).
• By Le Chatelier's principle / kinetic theory, adding heat shifts equilibrium to the left, decreasing gas solubility.
• Expelled dissolved air nucleates on microscopic imperfections on the beaker wall!
