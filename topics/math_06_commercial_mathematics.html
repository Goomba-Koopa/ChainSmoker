Commercial Mathematics: Percentages, Profit & Loss & Simple Interest

Financial foundations, commercial percentage calculations, profit margins, retail discounts, and simple interest accumulation.

The Grade 6 Bridge

From Grade 6 Intuition to Grade 7 Reality

Mathematics builds layer upon layer. Here is how Grade 6 price tags evolve into Grade 7 financial accounting:
Grade 6: The Simple Model Flat Counter Prices & Fixed Discounts
  • Isolated Purchase Prices: Cost Price was simply the sticker price paid at purchase; additional transport or repair costs were not factored in.
  • Direct Price Reductions: Discounts were treated as simple flat subtraction without considering retail markup chains (\(\text{CP} \to \text{MP} \to \text{SP}\)).
  • Flat Loan Fees: Borrowing money was viewed through flat compensation fees, without continuous annual rates of interest (\(R\%\text{ p.a.}\)) scaled over time.
Grade 7: The Deeper Reality Overhead Accounting & Time-Scaled Interest
  • Effective Cost Accounting: Overheads are strictly capitalized: \(\text{Effective CP} = \text{Purchase Price} + \text{Repairs} + \text{Freight}\). Profit% is strictly \(\frac{\text{Profit}}{\text{Effective CP}} \times 100\%\).
  • Strict Base Non-Interchangeability: Profit% and Loss% are never calculated on Selling Price. Discount percentage is strictly computed against Marked Price (MP).
  • Annualized Simple Interest: The capital interest law \(I = \frac{PRT}{100}\) demands that time \(T\) must be measured strictly in years (\(m\text{ months} = \frac{m}{12}\text{ yr}\)).

1. The Currency of Comparison: Percentages

Suppose Student A scores 18 out of 20 on a physics quiz, while Student B scores 42 out of 50 on a chemistry exam. Who demonstrated superior mastery? Raw marks cannot be compared because the base scales differ. We need a universal common denominator.

That universal denominator is the Latin per centum ("by the hundred"). A percentage standardizes every ratio into an equivalent fraction out of 100.

Percentage A fraction whose denominator is strictly 100. Represented by the symbol %: $$x\% = \frac{x}{100}$$ To convert any fraction or decimal to a percentage, multiply by 100%. To convert a percentage back to a fraction or decimal, divide by 100.
Percentage Increase & Decrease Principle
When a quantity changes from an original initial value \(V_0\) to a final value \(V_1\), the absolute change is \(\Delta V = |V_1 - V_0|\).
The relative rate of growth or decay is strictly measured against the original base value: $$\text{Percentage Increase} = \frac{\text{Absolute Increase}}{\text{Original Value}} \times 100\%$$ $$\text{Percentage Decrease} = \frac{\text{Absolute Decrease}}{\text{Original Value}} \times 100\%$$
Heads-Up • Successive Percentage Changes & Changing Bases

If a product's price rises by 20% and subsequently decreases by 20%, it does not return to its original price.
Let the initial price be \(\text{Rs } 100\).
After a 20% increase: \(100 + 20 = \text{Rs } 120\).
The subsequent 20% decrease applies to the new base of \(\text{Rs } 120\): \(20\% \times 120 = \text{Rs } 24\).
Final price: \(120 - 24 = \text{Rs } 96\) (a net 4% decrease). Percentages calculated on different bases cannot simply be added or subtracted.

2. Commercial Ledgers: Cost Price, Selling Price & Profit Margins

Every commercial transaction balances two core variables: what it costs the merchant to acquire an item, and what the consumer pays for it.

Commercial Ledger Elements • Cost Price (CP): The total amount spent to purchase, manufacture, and transport an article (including overhead charges like shipping, repairs, and labor).
• Selling Price (SP): The price at which the merchant sells the article to the consumer.
• Marked Price (MP / List Price): The tag price printed on the article before any retail discounts are deducted.
Financial Condition Governing Formula Percentage Yield (Always on CP)
Profit (Gain)
\(SP > CP\)		 $$\text{Profit} = SP - CP$$ $$\text{Profit}\% = \frac{\text{Profit}}{CP} \times 100$$
Loss
\(CP > SP\)		 $$\text{Loss} = CP - SP$$ $$\text{Loss}\% = \frac{\text{Loss}}{CP} \times 100$$
Retail Discount
\(MP > SP\)		 $$\text{Discount} = MP - SP$$ $$\text{Discount}\% = \frac{\text{Discount}}{MP} \times 100$$
The Golden Rule of Profit & Loss

Unless explicitly instructed otherwise by a question, Profit Percentage and Loss Percentage are ALWAYS calculated with Cost Price (CP) as the denominator. Calculating profit percentage on Selling Price is an instant zero on board exams.

Conversely, Discount Percentage is ALWAYS calculated on Marked Price (MP), because discount is a reduction from the printed price tag.

3. The Rent of Capital: Simple Interest

When you borrow an apartment, you pay rent. When you borrow money from a bank or lender, you pay rent on that capital. That financial rent is termed Interest.

Simple Interest Variables • Principal (P): The original sum of money deposited, invested, or borrowed.
• Rate of Interest (R): The annual percentage fee charged on the principal (e.g., \(8\%\) per annum or p.a.).
• Time (T): The period for which the money is borrowed, strictly expressed in years.
• Amount (A): The total monetary sum returned at the end of the term (Principal + Accumulated Interest).
The Fundamental Simple Interest Formula
For \(Rs\; 100\), the interest for 1 year is \(Rs\; R\).
For \(Rs\; 1\), the interest for 1 year is \(\frac{R}{100}\).
For principal \(P\), the interest for 1 year is \(P \times \frac{R}{100}\).
Over a duration of \(T\) years, the total simple interest \(I\) scales linearly: $$I = \frac{P \times R \times T}{100}$$
The total maturity Amount \(A\) paid back to the lender is: $$A = P + I = P + \frac{PRT}{100} = P\left(1 + \frac{RT}{100}\right)$$
Heads-Up • Time Units in Simple Interest

The time variable \(T\) in \(I = \frac{PRT}{100}\) must always be expressed in years:
• If given in months (e.g., 9 months): \(T = \frac{9}{12} = \frac{3}{4}\text{ years}\).
• If given in days (e.g., 73 days): \(T = \frac{73}{365} = \frac{1}{5}\text{ year}\).

4. Worked Problem• ICSE Examination Problems

Problem 6.1 • Profit and Loss Finding Selling Price from Percentage Profit
A shopkeeper buys a bicycle for Rs 3,200 and spends Rs 300 on transport and repainting. If he sells it at a profit of 15%, calculate his net selling price.
Step 1: Calculate total effective Cost Price (CP).
Overhead costs must be added to purchase price:
$$\text{Total } CP = Rs\; 3,200 + Rs\; 300 = Rs\; 3,500$$
Step 2: Compute monetary profit.
$$\text{Profit} = 15\% \text{ of } CP = \frac{15}{100} \times 3,500 = 15 \times 35 = Rs\; 525$$
Step 3: Determine Selling Price (SP).
$$SP = CP + \text{Profit} = Rs\; 3,500 + Rs\; 525 = Rs\; 4,025$$
Final Answer: The selling price of the bicycle is Rs 4,025.
Problem 6.2 • Retail Discounts Finding Marked Price Given Discount Rate
A cricket bat is sold for Rs 1,440 after allowing a discount of 20% on its Marked Price. Determine the original Marked Price of the bat.
Step 1: Express selling price as a percentage of marked price.
If discount is 20%, the customer pays \(100\% - 20\% = 80\%\) of the Marked Price (MP).
$$SP = 80\% \text{ of } MP = 0.80 \times MP$$
Step 2: Set up equation and solve for MP.
$$0.80 \times MP = 1,440$$ $$MP = \frac{1,440}{0.80} = \frac{14,400}{8} = Rs\; 1,800$$
Final Answer: The Marked Price of the cricket bat is Rs 1,800.
Problem 6.3 • Simple Interest Principal Determination from Maturity Amount
What principal sum of money invested at 8% per annum simple interest will accumulate to a total amount of Rs 7,440 in 3 years?
Step 1: Set up the Amount formula in terms of Principal \(P\).
$$A = P + I = P + \frac{P \times R \times T}{100}$$ $$7,440 = P + \frac{P \times 8 \times 3}{100} = P + \frac{24P}{100}$$
Step 2: Combine terms over common denominator.
$$7,440 = \frac{100P + 24P}{100} = \frac{124P}{100}$$
Step 3: Isolate \(P\).
$$P = \frac{7,440 \times 100}{124}$$ Notice: \(124 \times 6 = 744\), so \(7,440 / 124 = 60\).
$$P = 60 \times 100 = Rs\; 6,000$$
Final Answer: The principal invested was Rs 6,000.

5. Scientific & Economic Reasoning

Curiosity 6.1: Why Does Simple Interest Grow Linearly?
Why is simple interest called "simple", and why does the interest earned in Year 10 equal the interest earned in Year 1?
Simple interest is termed "simple" because the interest earned in each time period is calculated solely and perpetually on the original initial principal.

The interest accumulated in year 1 does not join the capital to generate further interest (that would be Compound Interest, studied in Class 8). Because the base capital remains frozen at \(P\), the yearly interest is constant: \(\Delta I = \frac{P \times R}{100}\) every single year.
Curiosity 6.2: The Marketing Psychology of Marked Prices
Why do retail merchants deliberately inflate marked prices before advertising massive "40% OFF" sales?
This is known as price anchoring. If an article costs \(Rs\; 600\) to buy (CP), a merchant might want a \(Rs\; 300\) profit (selling at \(Rs\; 900\)).

Instead of listing the price as \(Rs\; 900\), the merchant marks the price up to \(Rs\; 1,500\) and offers a "40% discount" (\(40\% \text{ of } 1,500 = Rs\; 600\) off, leaving \(SP = Rs\; 900\)). The consumer feels they saved \(Rs\; 600\), while the merchant achieves their full \(50\%\) profit margin!
Exam Strategy • The 5-Second Filter

8. The Common-Sense Strategy: Financial Reality Checks

Elimination Drill
"Common sense is the law." Commercial arithmetic governs real commerce. Money spent on repairs is true expenditure from your pocket; interest for a few months cannot dwarf the initial principal loan. Before launching into arithmetic, inspect the options against financial reality and accounting principles.
Check 01 The Overhead Capitalization Test

Never ignore secondary costs. If an asset is purchased and refurbished, effective \(\text{CP} = \text{Purchase} + \text{Repairs}\). Any profit percentage calculated solely on the purchase price is artificially inflated and invalid.

Check 02 The Base Denominator Rule

Profit% and Loss% are strictly measured against Cost Price: \(\frac{\text{Profit}}{\text{CP}} \times 100\%\). Disqualify any trap options generated by incorrectly dividing profit by the Selling Price (\(\text{SP}\)).

Check 03 The Simple Interest Time Unit (\(T\) in Years)

Because rates are declared per annum (\(\%\text{ p.a.}\)), time \(T\) must be expressed in years. If a question states 8 months, substituting \(T = 8\) yields twelve times the real interest! Check that \(T = \frac{8}{12} = \frac{2}{3}\).

Examiner's Assessment: Fatal Substitution Error
The interest rate is stated per annum (per year). Time must strictly be in years! Kabir plugged in \(T = 8\) as if it were 8 years. For 8 months, \(T = \frac{8}{12} = \frac{2}{3}\text{ year}\). True interest is \(\frac{4500 \times 8 \times 2}{100 \times 3} = \text{Rs } 240\). Kabir's answer of \(\text{Rs } 2880\) is 12 times too high! 0 marks.
⚠ Error Carried Forward (ECF) Score: 1/1 Mark
Examiner's Assessment: Method Credit Conferred
Although the interest figure is incorrect, Kabir correctly applied the definition of total amount \(A = P + I\) to his calculated values without additional arithmetic errors. ICSE awards 1 method mark under ECF.
Step 1: Standardize Time to Years [1 Mark]

Principal \(P = \text{Rs } 4500\), Rate \(R = 8\%\text{ per annum}\).
Since the rate is per annum, time \(T\) must be converted from months to years:
\[T = \frac{8}{12}\text{ year} = \frac{2}{3}\text{ year}\]

Step 2: Simple Interest Calculation [1 Mark]

Apply the simple interest formula:
\[I = \frac{P \times R \times T}{100} = \frac{4500 \times 8 \times \frac{2}{3}}{100} = \frac{4500 \times 8 \times 2}{100 \times 3}\]
Simplifying:
\[I = 15 \times 16 = \mathbf{\text{Rs } 240}\]

Step 3: Total Amount Payable [1 Mark]

Compute total amount due:
\[A = P + I = 4500 + 240 = \mathbf{\text{Rs } 4740}\]

Chief Examiner's Tip: In ICSE commercial math problems, always circle the time period and the rate unit. If the rate is p.a., time in months must be divided by 12, and time in days must be divided by 365. Never substitute months directly into \(I = \frac{PRT}{100}\)!
