Motion, Distance, Displacement & Speed

The relative nature of rest and motion, translatory particle mechanics (rectilinear vs curvilinear), rotatory and periodic systems, scalar distance vs vector displacement, uniform vs non-uniform speed, and algebraic conversion proofs.

The Grade 6 Bridge

From Grade 6 Intuition to Grade 7 Reality

Physics builds layer upon layer. Here is how what you learned in Grade 6 evolves into Grade 7 mechanics:
Grade 6: The Simple Model Scalar counting and simple division
  • Speed was just a formula: You calculated speed as \(\text{Speed} = \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Time}}\) to measure how fast something moved.
  • Movement was an odometer count: Any step forward, backward, or in loops simply added to the total distance covered. Direction was not tracked.
  • Everything was one-dimensional: Speeds were typically compared in identical units without needing dimensional cancellation proofs.
Grade 7: The Deeper Reality Vectors, reference frames, and dimensional fractions
  • Direction is paramount: We separate scalar distance (path length) from vector displacement (the shortest straight line from origin to final position). Returning to your start point always yields \(\vec{s} = 0\).
  • Motion is relative to an observer: You must always specify a reference frame. A seated train passenger is stationary relative to the train carriage, but moving at \(72\text{ km/h}\) relative to a station lamp post.
  • The 5/18 dimensional shortcut: We derive the exact ratio \(\frac{1000\text{ m}}{3600\text{ s}} = \frac{5}{18}\text{ m/s}\), allowing you to convert between \(\text{km/h}\) and \(\text{m/s}\) in a single algebraic multiplication.

1. The Relativity of Rest and Motion

Rest & Motion Postulate: A body is said to be at rest if it does not change its position with respect to a fixed reference point (surroundings) with time. A body is in motion if its position continuously changes with respect to its surroundings.

• Crucial Examiner Insight: Rest and motion are purely relative terms. Absolute rest or absolute motion does not exist in the physical universe. A passenger seated inside a moving railway carriage is at rest relative to co-passengers and the carriage walls, yet in rapid motion relative to trees, telephone poles, and platforms outside.
The Role of the Reference Frame (Origin of Coordinates)

To state whether an object is moving, an observer must establish a Reference Frame — a stationary reference point (often designated the origin \(O\)) against which position vectors are measured. Without defining a reference point, describing motion is mathematically and physically meaningless.

2. Translatory Motion: Particle Mechanics & Geometry

Before comparing complex forms of motion, we must establish the precise mechanical definition of Translatory Motion (also termed pure translation).

Translatory Motion Criterion: A rigid body is said to possess Translatory Motion if every constituent particle of the body shifts through the exact same distance along parallel trajectories during any given time interval.

Geometric Property: If a straight line segment \(AB\) is drawn connecting any two arbitrary points inside the moving body, the segment \(AB\) remains strictly parallel to its initial orientation at every single instant of time (\(AB \parallel A'B'\)).

Translatory motion is divided into two fundamental geometric classes depending on the path traced in space:

Parameter Rectilinear Translatory Motion Curvilinear Translatory Motion
Path Trajectory Motion occurs along a straight line path. Motion occurs along a curved path in two or three dimensions.
Particle Trajectories All particles trace parallel straight lines of identical length. All particles trace parallel curved paths of identical shape and length.
Orientation of Body Fixed direction; line segment \(AB \parallel A'B'\). Fixed orientation; the body does not rotate as it traverses the curve.
Real-World Examples • A coin sliding freely on a smooth horizontal carrom board.
• A stone dropped vertically from a height under gravity.
• A locomotive running on a straight, unbanked railway track. 		• A car navigating a curved road bend without spinning on its axis.
• A stone thrown horizontally from a cliff tracing a parabolic trajectory.
• A child sliding down a smooth curved park slide.
Translatory vs. Rotatory: The Critical Mechanical Distinction

In pure Translatory Motion, all particles possess identical velocities and undergo equal displacements in equal time intervals. In contrast, in pure Rotatory Motion, the body turns around a fixed internal or external axis: points lying at different radial distances from the axis trace concentric circular paths of different lengths with different linear speeds (\(v = \omega r\)), while points lying on the axis remain completely stationary.

3. Rotatory, Oscillatory, Periodic & Random Motion

Beyond translatory motion, mechanical systems exhibit rotational, oscillatory, and combined motions:

Type of Motion Defining Physical Mechanism Real-World Physical Examples
1. Rotatory Motion A body pivots around a fixed stationary axis passing through it. Different points describe concentric circular paths of varying radii. • Blades of a ceiling fan rotating about its central spindle.
• Earth spinning about its geographic polar axis (\(24\text{ hours}\)).
• The wheel of a stationary sewing machine.
2. Circular Motion An entire body (treated as a point mass) revolves along the circumference of a circular path around an external centre. • Motion of the Earth revolving around the Sun.
• A stone tied to a string whirled horizontally in a circle.
3. Oscillatory / Vibratory Motion A body moves repeatedly to-and-fro (back-and-forth) about a fixed central mean equilibrium position at regular intervals. • The swinging bob of a simple pendulum.
• Motion of a child on a playground swing.
• Vibrations of a struck tuning fork or plucked guitar string.
4. Periodic Motion Any motion that repeats itself in exact, identical intervals of time (Time Period \(T\)). • Revolution of the Moon around the Earth (\(27.3\text{ days}\)).
• Movement of the hands of a mechanical clock.
5. Non-Periodic Motion Motion that does not repeat at regular predictable time intervals. • Movement of an automobile in busy traffic.
• Swaying of tree branches in fluctuating wind.
6. Multiple / Combined Motion A single body executing two or more distinct types of motion simultaneously. • Rolling Bicycle Wheel: Translatory (linear road progress) + Rotatory (spinning on axle).
• Earth: Rotatory (spin) + Circular (orbital revolution).
• Drill Bit: Rotatory (drilling) + Translatory (forward penetration).

4. Distance vs. Displacement

Kinematics distinguishes between the total ground an object traverses and its net change in position:

Characteristic Distance (\(s\)) Displacement (\(\vec{s}\))
Definition Total length of the actual path traversed by a moving body irrespective of direction. Shortest straight-line distance measured from initial position to final position in a specified direction.
Quantity Type Scalar quantity (has magnitude only). Vector quantity (has both magnitude and specific direction).
Sign & Value Always positive; never zero or negative for a body in motion. Can be positive, negative, or zero depending on direction.
Magnitude Relationship \(\text{Distance} \ge |\text{Displacement}|\). Equal only in straight-line one-directional motion. \(|\text{Displacement}| \le \text{Distance}\). Cannot exceed distance.
Closed Loop Motion Equal to total perimeter (\(s = 2\pi r\) for a full circle). Exactly zero (\(\vec{s} = 0\)) because initial and final positions coincide.
SI & CGS Units SI: \(\text{metre (m)}\); CGS: \(\text{centimetre (cm)}\) SI: \(\text{metre (m)}\); CGS: \(\text{centimetre (cm)}\)

5. Speed Formulas & Conversion Derivations

Speed Definition & Formula: The speed of a body is the rate of distance covered per unit time: $$\text{Speed } (v) = \frac{\text{Distance } (s)}{\text{Time } (t)}$$ • Uniform Speed: When a body covers equal distances in equal intervals of time, however small the intervals may be.
• Non-Uniform (Variable) Speed: When a body covers unequal distances in equal intervals of time.
• Average Speed: $$v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\text{Total Distance Covered } (S_{\text{total}})}{\text{Total Time Taken } (T_{\text{total}})}$$
Mathematical Proof: \(1\text{ km/h} = \frac{5}{18}\text{ m/s}\)
1. By definition of standard metric conversion factors:
    \(1\text{ kilometre (km)} = 1,000\text{ metres (m)}\)
    \(1\text{ hour (h)} = 60\text{ minutes} \times 60\text{ seconds} = 3,600\text{ seconds (s)}\)

2. Taking the ratio of length over time:
    $$1\text{ km/h} = \frac{1,000\text{ m}}{3,600\text{ s}} = \frac{10}{36}\text{ m/s} = \frac{\mathbf{5}}{\mathbf{18}}\text{ m/s} \approx 0.2778\text{ m/s}$$
3. Conversion Rules:
    • To convert \(\text{km/h} \to \text{m/s}\): Multiply by \(\frac{5}{18}\).
    • To convert \(\text{m/s} \to \text{km/h}\): Multiply by \(\frac{18}{5} = 3.6\).

6. Worked Problems

Problem 1.1: Non-Uniform Round Journey & Average Speed [3 Marks]
A car travels a distance of \(120\text{ km}\) from City A to City B at a uniform speed of \(60\text{ km/h}\), and returns from City B to City A over the identical route at a speed of \(40\text{ km/h}\). Calculate: (i) Total time taken, and (ii) Average speed of the entire journey.
Step-by-Step Logical Resolution:

1. Forward Journey (A to B):
    \(t_1 = \frac{s_1}{v_1} = \frac{120\text{ km}}{60\text{ km/h}} = \mathbf{2.0\text{ hours}}\)

2. Return Journey (B to A):
    \(t_2 = \frac{s_2}{v_2} = \frac{120\text{ km}}{40\text{ km/h}} = \mathbf{3.0\text{ hours}}\)
    Total Time \(T_{\text{total}} = t_1 + t_2 = 2.0 + 3.0 = \mathbf{5.0\text{ hours}}\)

3. Average Speed:
    Total Distance \(S_{\text{total}} = 120 + 120 = 240\text{ km}\)
    $$v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{S_{\text{total}}}{T_{\text{total}}} = \frac{240\text{ km}}{5\text{ h}} = \mathbf{48\text{ km/h}} \quad \left(= 48 \times \frac{5}{18} = \mathbf{13.33\text{ m/s}}\right)$$
Key Physical Intuition: Average speed is not the simple arithmetic mean of 60 and 40 (\(50\text{ km/h}\)) because the vehicle spends more time traveling at the slower speed.
Problem 1.2: Circular Track Distance & Displacement [3 Marks]
An athlete runs along a circular track of radius \(r = 14\text{ m}\). Calculate the distance covered and the magnitude of displacement when the athlete completes: (i) Half a revolution, (ii) One full revolution. [Take \(\pi = \frac{22}{7}\)]
Step-by-Step Logical Resolution:

1. Case (i): Half a Revolution (Diameter opposite):
    • Distance \(s = \pi r = \frac{22}{7} \times 14\text{ m} = \mathbf{44\text{ m}}\)
    • Displacement \(\vec{s} = \text{Diameter} = 2r = 2 \times 14\text{ m} = \mathbf{28\text{ m}}\) directed from initial to opposite point.

2. Case (ii): One Full Revolution (Initial = Final position):
    • Distance \(s = 2\pi r = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 14\text{ m} = \mathbf{88\text{ m}}\)
    • Displacement \(\vec{s} = \mathbf{0\text{ m}}\) (since the initial and final position vectors are identical).
Problem 1.3: Metric Unit Conversion & Speed Comparison [2 Marks]
A cheetah sprints at \(90\text{ km/h}\), while a carrier pigeon flies at \(24\text{ m/s}\). By rigorous metric conversion, determine which moves faster and compute the difference in speed in \(\text{m/s}\).
Step-by-Step Logical Resolution:

1. Convert Cheetah's Speed to SI units (\(\text{m/s}\)):
    $$v_{\text{cheetah}} = 90 \times \frac{5}{18}\text{ m/s} = 5 \times 5\text{ m/s} = \mathbf{25\text{ m/s}}$$
2. Compare with Pigeon:
    \(v_{\text{pigeon}} = 24\text{ m/s}\)
    Since \(25\text{ m/s} > 24\text{ m/s}\), the cheetah is faster.

3. Speed Difference:
    $$\Delta v = 25\text{ m/s} - 24\text{ m/s} = \mathbf{1.0\text{ m/s}} \quad (= 3.6\text{ km/h})$$

7. Scientific Reasoning & Conceptual Q&A

Conceptual Deduction 1: Non-Zero Distance with Zero Displacement
Can a physical body have traversed a non-zero distance while its net displacement remains exactly zero? Provide a rigorous mechanical example.
Scientific Resolution:
Yes. Distance is a scalar quantity measuring the cumulative length of the actual path traversed, which continuously increases as long as the object moves (\(s > 0\)). Displacement is a vector defined as the shortest straight-line distance between the initial and final positions (\(\vec{s} = \vec{r}_f - \vec{r}_i\)).

Whenever a moving object returns to its starting point (a closed loop, such as a child completing a lap on a running track or a thrown ball returning to the thrower's hand), the initial and final position vectors coincide (\(\vec{r}_f = \vec{r}_i\)), making net displacement \(\vec{s} = 0\), even though distance \(s > 0\).
Conceptual Deduction 2: Straight-Line Curvilinear Particle Tracks
Why is the motion of a train rounding a horizontal curve classified as curvilinear translatory motion rather than rotatory motion?
Scientific Resolution:
In Curvilinear Translatory Motion, all particles of the train body shift through the exact same distance along parallel curved tracks in any given time interval, and the train does not turn continuously around an internal fixed axle.

In pure Rotatory Motion, an internal axis remains stationary while particles at different radial distances trace concentric circles with different speeds. Since every passenger and carriage on the train moves along parallel curved paths with equal speeds, its overall movement is curvilinear translation.
Exam Strategy • The 5-Second Filter

8. The Common-Sense Strategy: Rule Out the Impossible

Elimination Drill
"Common sense is the law." In physics and mathematics, equations are not abstract strings of numbers—they describe physical reality. Top problem solvers don't blindly grind algebra right away; they inspect the options first and ask: Which of these are physically absurd or mathematically impossible? In multiple-choice questions, you can frequently rule out 2 or 3 options in under 5 seconds.
Check 01 The Reality & Scale Check

Does the magnitude make physical sense? A suburban local train cannot move near Mach 1 (speed of sound); a running human cannot hit \(150\text{ km/h}\); elapsed time and scalar speed can never be negative or zero for moving bodies.

Check 02 The Boundary Bracket Rule

Average speed must always lie strictly between the slowest and fastest speeds (\(v_{\min} < v_{\text{avg}} < v_{\max}\)). Any option higher than the peak speed or lower than the minimum speed is an immediate physical impossibility.

Check 03 The Slow-Leg Trap

Because \(t = \frac{s}{v}\), covering equal distances at different speeds means spending more time at the slower speed. Therefore, the average speed is always pulled below the simple midpoint (\(\frac{v_1+v_2}{2}\)).

The car burned petrol and traveled 240 real kilometers—speed can NEVER be zero for an object that moved! (Only average velocity is zero).
B \(75\text{ km/h}\)

⚡ Ruled Out in 2 Seconds Boundary Bracket Violation
Why common sense eliminates this: The car's maximum speed during any part of the journey was \(60\text{ km/h}\). How could the overall average speed possibly be \(75\text{ km/h}\)? An average can never exceed the highest speed recorded (\(v_{\text{avg}} \le v_{\max}\)). Disqualified immediately!
C \(50\text{ km/h}\)

⚡ Ruled Out in 5 Seconds The Arithmetic Midpoint Trap
Why common sense eliminates this: \(50\text{ km/h}\) is the simple arithmetic mean \(\frac{60 + 40}{2}\). But the car spent 3 full hours traveling at the slower speed of \(40\text{ km/h}\) (\(\frac{120}{40} = 3\text{ h}\)) and only 2 hours at \(60\text{ km/h}\) (\(\frac{120}{60} = 2\text{ h}\)). Because more journey time was spent at the slower speed, the average speed must be pulled below 50 km/h!

Click to test with common sense ⚡
✓ The Only Survivor! Physical & Mathematical Confirmation
Why common sense picks this without calculating: It is the ONLY option that lies strictly inside the bracket \((40, 60)\) and is correctly weighted below the \(50\text{ km/h}\) midpoint!

The 10-Second Mathematical Proof:
• Total distance = \(120\text{ km} + 120\text{ km} = 240\text{ km}\).
• Total time = \(2.0\text{ h} + 3.0\text{ h} = 5.0\text{ h}\).
• Average speed = \(\frac{240\text{ km}}{5.0\text{ h}} = \mathbf{48\text{ km/h}}\). You ruled out 3 options in seconds without writing a single algebraic equation!
