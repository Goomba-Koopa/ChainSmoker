Physics • Topic 05

Electricity: Current, Circuits & Effects

Electric current (I = Q/t), potential difference, series vs parallel circuits, Ohm's Law, Joule heating (H = I²Rt), fuses, and the magnetic effect of current.

1. Electric Current and Potential Difference

When you flip a light switch, a bulb instantly lights up. This happens because billions of tiny, invisible particles called electrons rush through the copper wires, much like water rushing through a garden hose. However, water doesn't flow through a hose on its own; it needs a water pump to push it. Similarly, electrons need an electrical "pump"—like a battery or a generator—to push them through a circuit. The flow of these electrons is called electric current, and the push provided by the battery is the potential difference.

Electric Current (\(I\)): The rate of flow of electric charge (\(Q\)) through any cross-section of a conductor in a unit time (\(t\)).
Formula: \(I = \frac{Q}{t}\)
SI Unit: Ampere (A), where \(1\text{ A} = 1\text{ Coulomb per second (C/s)}\).
Potential Difference (\(V\)): The amount of work done in moving a unit positive charge from one point to another in an electric circuit. It acts as the driving force that causes charges to flow.
SI Unit: Volt (V), where \(1\text{ V} = 1\text{ Joule per Coulomb (J/C)}\).

2. Ohm's Law and Resistance

Imagine squeezing a water hose: the water flow slows down because the squeeze creates resistance. In an electrical circuit, the wire itself (or components like bulbs) provides resistance to the flow of electrons. In 1827, the German physicist Georg Simon Ohm discovered a fundamental relationship between the "push" (voltage), the "flow" (current), and the "squeeze" (resistance).

Ohm's Law: Ohm's Law states that the physical conditions (like temperature) remaining constant, the current (\(I\)) flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference (\(V\)) applied across its ends.
Formula: \(V = I \times R\), where \(R\) is the Resistance of the conductor.
SI Unit of Resistance: Ohm (\(\Omega\)).
Factors Affecting Resistance (\(R\))
The resistance of a wire depends on several physical factors:

1. Length (\(l\)): Resistance is directly proportional to length (\(R \propto l\)). A longer wire has more resistance.
2. Area of Cross-Section (\(A\)): Resistance is inversely proportional to the thickness (\(R \propto \frac{1}{A}\)). A thicker wire has less resistance.
3. Nature of Material: Copper has low resistance, while nichrome has high resistance.
4. Temperature: For most pure metals, resistance increases with an increase in temperature.

3. Series vs. Parallel Circuits

Think of old decorative string lights: if one bulb burns out, the entire string goes dark because there is only one continuous path for the electricity. This is a series circuit. In contrast, the electrical outlets in your house are wired so that turning off the television doesn't turn off the refrigerator. This multiple-pathway setup is a parallel circuit.

Characteristic Series Circuit Parallel Circuit
Path for Current Only one continuous path; current is the same everywhere. Multiple branches; current divides among the branches.
Failure Impact If one component fails, the entire circuit is broken. If one component fails, other branches continue working normally.
Equivalent Resistance (\(R_{eq}\)) Sum of individual resistances: \(R_{eq} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3\) Reciprocal sum: \(\frac{1}{R_{eq}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3}\)
Potential Difference (Voltage) Voltage is divided among components (\(V = V_1 + V_2 + V_3\)). Voltage is identical across all parallel branches (\(V = V_1 = V_2 = V_3\)).

4. Heating and Magnetic Effects of Current

When you look inside a working toaster, you see glowing red wires. This happens because electrical resistance generates heat as electrons collide with atoms in the wire. Conversely, when current flows through a wire coiled around a piece of iron, it turns the iron into a strong magnet—this is the principle used in giant scrap-yard cranes to lift heavy cars.

Joule's Law of Heating: The heat (\(H\)) produced in a resistor is directly proportional to the square of the current (\(I^2\)), the resistance (\(R\)), and the time (\(t\)) for which the current flows.
Formula: \(H = I^2Rt\)
Note: This principle is applied in electric irons, heaters, and fuses.
Electric Fuse: A safety device placed in an electrical circuit consisting of a short piece of wire (often an alloy of tin and lead) with a very low melting point. If the current exceeds a safe limit, Joule heating melts the wire instantly, breaking the circuit and preventing fire.
Electromagnetism: A magnetic field is created around a current-carrying conductor. An electromagnet is a temporary magnet made by winding a coil of insulated wire (solenoid) around a soft iron core. It loses its magnetism as soon as the current is switched off.

5. Worked Problems

Problem 5.1: Ohm's Law and Power [3 Marks]
A resistor of \(8\text{ }\Omega\) carries a current of \(3\text{ A}\). Calculate (i) the potential difference (voltage) across it, and (ii) the energy (power dissipated) in 10 minutes.
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. Part (i): Potential Difference
    Given: Resistance \(R = 8\text{ }\Omega\), Current \(I = 3\text{ A}\).
    Using Ohm's Law: \(V = I \times R\)
    \(V = 3\text{ A} \times 8\text{ }\Omega = \mathbf{24\text{ V}}\) [1 Mark]

2. Part (ii): Energy Dissipated (Joule Heating)
    First, calculate the Power (\(P\)): \(P = I^2R\) or \(P = V \times I\).
    \(P = 24\text{ V} \times 3\text{ A} = 72\text{ W (Joules per second)}\) [1 Mark]
    Time \(t = 10\text{ minutes} = 10 \times 60\text{ s} = 600\text{ s}\).
    Energy \(H = P \times t = 72\text{ W} \times 600\text{ s} = \mathbf{43,200\text{ Joules (or 43.2 kJ)}}\) [1 Mark]
Problem 5.2: Equivalent Resistance in Parallel [4 Marks]
Three resistors of \(2\text{ }\Omega\), \(3\text{ }\Omega\), and \(6\text{ }\Omega\) are connected in parallel across a \(12\text{ V}\) battery. Find (i) the total equivalent resistance of the circuit, and (ii) the total current drawn from the battery.
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. Part (i): Total Equivalent Resistance (\(R_{eq}\))
    For parallel connection: \(\frac{1}{R_{eq}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3}\)
    \(\frac{1}{R_{eq}} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{6}\)
    Taking the lowest common multiple (6):
    \(\frac{1}{R_{eq}} = \frac{3 + 2 + 1}{6} = \frac{6}{6} = 1\)
    Therefore, \(R_{eq} = \mathbf{1\text{ }\Omega}\) [2 Marks]

2. Part (ii): Total Current (\(I\))
    Given Total Voltage \(V = 12\text{ V}\).
    Using Ohm's Law for the whole circuit: \(I = \frac{V}{R_{eq}}\)
    \(I = \frac{12\text{ V}}{1\text{ }\Omega} = \mathbf{12\text{ A}}\) [2 Marks]

6. Give Scientific Reasons

Q1: Why is tungsten used for the filament of an electric bulb?
Tungsten has a very high melting point (approx 3380°C) and high resistance. When electric current passes through it, it becomes white-hot without melting, thereby emitting bright light.
Q2: Why are appliances in a household connected in parallel rather than in series?
In a parallel circuit, if one appliance breaks down or is switched off, the others continue to operate independently because each has its own closed path to the power supply. Additionally, all appliances receive the full standard mains voltage.
Q3: Why is a fuse wire made of a tin-lead alloy?
A tin-lead alloy has a notably low melting point. If a short circuit causes excessive current to flow, Joule heating (\(H = I^2Rt\)) instantly raises the temperature, melting the fuse wire and breaking the circuit before expensive electrical appliances are damaged or a fire starts.

8. The Common-Sense Strategy: Reality Checks

Elimination Drill
"Common sense is the law." In science and mathematics, formulas describe physical reality. Top problem solvers don't blindly calculate; they inspect the options first and ask: Which of these are physically absurd or mathematically impossible? Use your physical instincts to eliminate wrong answers before writing down a single equation.
Check 01 The Reality Scale Check

Does the magnitude make physical sense? A biological cell cannot be 1 meter long, and an everyday chemical reaction cannot yield millions of liters. Always check if the value fits the real-world scale.

Check 02 The Boundary Bracket Rule

Look for bounds. An average or a mixture must always lie strictly between the minimum and maximum inputs. Any option outside this bracket is an immediate physical impossibility.

Check 03 The Units & Dimension Check

Are the units correct? If the question asks for a rate, the answer cannot be in kilograms. Often, two options can be eliminated instantly just by looking at the trailing units.
