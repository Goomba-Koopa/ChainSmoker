Atomic Structure & Valency

The architecture of the atom: why atoms are mostly empty space, the Bohr shell model and the 2n² seating plan, why elements desperately borrow or dump electrons to achieve the noble gas octet, and the foolproof criss-cross method for writing chemical formulas.

The Grade 6 Bridge

From Grade 6 Intuition to Grade 7 Reality

Chemistry builds layer upon layer. Here is how what you learned in Grade 6 evolves into Grade 7 atomic architecture:
Grade 6: The Simple Model Indivisible spheres and element symbols
  • Atoms were indivisible: Dalton's billiard-ball model: atoms were tiny, solid spheres that could not be split or penetrated.
  • All atoms of an element were identical: Every carbon atom was assumed to have the exact same mass and properties.
  • Combination was mysterious: Atoms joined to form compounds without explaining why certain elements combine in fixed ratios while others never react.
Grade 7: The Deeper Reality Subatomic structure, energy shells, and octet stability
  • The Nuclear Atom is Empty Space: A dense central nucleus (\(p^+ + n^0\)) contains \(99.9\%\) of the mass, orbited by electrons in discrete shells (\(K, L, M\)) following \(2n^2\).
  • Nuclide Arithmetic & Isotopes: Atomic number (\(Z\)) defines protons; Mass number (\(A\)) equals \(p + n\). Isotopes (e.g. \(^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}\) and \(^{37}_{17}\text{Cl}\)) share \(Z\) but differ in neutron count.
  • The Drive for Octet Stability: Atoms react to achieve stable noble gas electron arrangements (\(8\) valence electrons). Valency is the exact number of electrons lost, gained, or shared.

Introduction: The Cricket Stadium in a Grain of Sand

For centuries, early scientists pictured atoms like hard little billiard balls—solid, indivisible, and indestructible. If that were true, chemistry would be pretty boring. Nothing could react, fuse, or transform.

The truth is far stranger. An atom is not solid at all. In fact, an atom is 99.999999999% completely empty space.

To picture this, imagine the Wankhede Cricket Stadium in Mumbai. If you blow up the atom so the entire stadium represents the outer boundary where the electrons buzz around, the dense central nucleus (holding all the protons and neutrons) is the size of a single tiny marble sitting right on the center of the pitch!

Everything else—the pitch, the boundary rope, the stands—is empty vacuum. Yet that tiny marble controls the atom's identity and mass, while the buzzing electrons at the stadium edge determine every single chemical reaction in the universe.

1. The Three Subatomic Particles

Every atom in existence—whether it sits in your smartphone, a leaf of grass, or a distant star—is assembled from just three fundamental building blocks:

The Three Subatomic Particles • Proton (\(p^+\)): Carries a positive electrical charge (\(+1\)) and sits locked inside the central nucleus. Relative mass = \(1\text{ amu}\).
• Neutron (\(n^0\)): Carries zero electrical charge (neutral) and acts as the nuclear glue between protons. Relative mass \(\approx 1\text{ amu}\).
• Electron (\(e^-\)): Carries a negative electrical charge (\(-1\)) and zips around the nucleus in designated energy orbits. Its mass is so ridiculously tiny (\(\approx \frac{1}{1837}\text{ amu}\)) that we treat it as virtually weightless!
Particle Symbol Electrical Charge Mass Comparison Where Does It Live?
Proton \(p^+\) \(+1\) (Positive) \(1\text{ amu}\) (Heavy) Inside the central nucleus
Neutron \(n^0\) \(0\) (Neutral / No charge) \(1\text{ amu}\) (Heavy) Inside the central nucleus
Electron \(e^-\) \(-1\) (Negative) Almost weightless (\(\approx 0\text{ amu}\)) Revolving in outer shells (orbits)
Why Don't You Get an Electric Shock Touching a Table?

If atoms are packed with charged protons and electrons, why don't objects zap you with electricity?

Because in any normal atom, the number of positive protons exactly equals the number of negative electrons. The positive charges cancel out the negative charges completely, making the whole atom electrically neutral (\(\text{Net Charge} = 0\)).

2. Atomic Number (\(Z\)) and Mass Number (\(A\))

Students constantly mix up Atomic Number and Mass Number in exams. Here is the easiest way to keep them straight:

  • Atomic Number (\(Z\)) is the ID Card: It counts only the protons. Every single atom with 6 protons is Carbon. Every atom with 8 protons is Oxygen. If you change the proton count, you fundamentally change the element! In a neutral atom, \(Z = \text{protons} = \text{electrons}\).
  • Mass Number (\(A\)) is the Weighing Scale: Since electrons weigh practically nothing, the entire weight of the atom comes from the heavy stuff packed inside the nucleus: Protons + Neutrons.
The Three Golden Formulas
1. Atomic Number (\(Z\)):
$$Z = \text{Number of Protons} = \text{Number of Electrons (in neutral atom)}$$
2. Mass Number (\(A\)):
$$A = \text{Protons} + \text{Neutrons} = Z + n$$
3. Finding Neutrons (The Subtraction Rule):
$$n = A - Z \quad (\text{Top number minus bottom number!})$$
4. Standard Notation:
$$_{Z}^{A}\text{X} \quad \text{e.g., } _{11}^{23}\text{Na}$$ For Sodium: Atomic Number \(Z = 11\) (11 protons, 11 electrons). Mass Number \(A = 23\).
Neutrons = \(23 - 11 = \mathbf{12\text{ neutrons}}\).
Heads-Up • Atomic Number vs Mass Number Placement

Remember: Mass Number (\(A\)) is always on TOP because mass is heavy and floats up like a hot air balloon, while Atomic Number (\(Z\)) is at the BOTTOM: $$_{Z}^{A}\text{X} \quad \text{Example: } _{6}^{12}\text{C}$$ Always write \(_{6}^{12}\text{C}\) and avoid inverting atomic and mass numbers on• ICSE examinations.

3. Bohr's Shell Model: The Seating Plan of the Atom

Electrons don't just buzz around in chaos. They are strictly organized into circular energy levels called shells (named K, L, M, N... starting from the closest to the nucleus).

Think of an airplane where passengers must fill the front rows before boarding the back rows. Danish physicist Niels Bohr showed that each shell has a strict maximum passenger capacity calculated by the formula **\(2n^2\)**, where \(n\) is the shell number:

Shell Letter Shell Number (\(n\)) Formula (\(2n^2\)) Maximum Passenger Capacity
K shell (Innermost) \(n = 1\) \(2 \times (1)^2 = 2 \times 1\) 2 electrons
L shell (2nd) \(n = 2\) \(2 \times (2)^2 = 2 \times 4\) 8 electrons
M shell (3rd) \(n = 3\) \(2 \times (3)^2 = 2 \times 9\) 18 electrons
N shell (4th) \(n = 4\) \(2 \times (4)^2 = 2 \times 16\) 32 electrons
The Crucial "Octet Rule" Exception for Class 7!

Notice that the M shell can mathematically hold up to 18 electrons. But here is the golden rule of chemistry:

No atom can ever hold more than 8 electrons in its outermost shell!

This is called the Octet Rule. Even if a shell has room for 18, once it reaches 8 electrons as the outer boundary, it becomes temporarily full and the next electrons are forced to spill over into the next shell. That is why Calcium (\(Z=20\)) is arranged as 2, 8, 8, 2 and NOT 2, 8, 10!

4. Valency: Why Do Atoms React?

Why does iron rust? Why does sodium explode in water? Why does gold stay shiny for thousands of years?

The answer comes down to one thing: atoms are desperately seeking peace and quiet.

The most stable elements in the universe are the Noble Gases (Helium, Neon, Argon). They have completely full outer shells (2 electrons for Helium, 8 electrons for Neon and Argon). Because their outer shells are full, they are happy, peaceful, and never react with anyone. They are the chemical VIPs.

Every other element on the periodic table is jealous of the noble gases. They will do whatever it takes to get 8 electrons in their outer shell—whether that means losing extra electrons, gaining missing electrons, or sharing electrons with a neighbor!

What is Valency? Valency is the combining capacity of an element. It is simply the number of electrons an atom needs to lose, gain, or share to achieve a stable outer shell of 8 electrons (an octet, or a duplet of 2 for light elements).

The Fast 3-Step Valency Shortcut

  1. Step 1: Write the electronic configuration (e.g., Sodium \(Z=11 \to \mathbf{2, 8, 1}\)).
  2. Step 2: Look at the outermost electrons (valence electrons).
  3. Step 3: Ask yourself: is it easier to dump them, or find more?
    • If outer electrons are 1, 2, or 3 (Metals): It's way easier to dump 1, 2, or 3 electrons than to hunt down 5, 6, or 7. So they lose them!
      $$\text{Valency} = \text{Number of outer electrons}$$ Example: Sodium (\(2, 8, 1\)) dumps 1 electron \(\to \mathbf{\text{Valency} = 1}\). Magnesium (\(2, 8, 2\)) dumps 2 \(\to \mathbf{\text{Valency} = 2}\).
    • If outer electrons are 5, 6, or 7 (Non-metals): It's way easier to steal or borrow 1, 2, or 3 electrons to reach 8.
      $$\text{Valency} = 8 - \text{Number of outer electrons}$$ Example: Chlorine (\(2, 8, 7\)) needs 1 electron \(\to 8 - 7 = \mathbf{\text{Valency} = 1}\). Oxygen (\(2, 6\)) needs 2 electrons \(\to 8 - 6 = \mathbf{\text{Valency} = 2}\).
    • If outer electrons are 4 (like Carbon \(2, 4\)): Losing 4 or gaining 4 is too hard, so it shares 4 electrons \(\to \mathbf{\text{Valency} = 4}\).
    • If outer electrons are 8 (Noble gases): Already full and happy! \(\to \mathbf{\text{Valency} = 0}\).
Heads-Up • Valency vs Ion Charge

Valency is a pure number with no plus or minus sign. It represents combining capacity. The ion charge carries a sign (\(\text{Na}^+\), \(\text{Mg}^{2+}\), \(\text{Cl}^-\)), but valency is written as plain 1, 2, or 3.

5. Writing Chemical Formulas: The Criss-Cross Dance

When elements combine to form a compound, the total positive valency must balance the total negative valency so the compound ends up electrically neutral. Instead of doing complicated balancing in your head, chemists use the famous Criss-Cross Method:

The 4-Step Criss-Cross Rule
  1. Step 1: Write down the symbols side-by-side (Positive metal first, negative non-metal second).
  2. Step 2: Write their valencies directly above their symbols.
  3. Step 3: Cross over the valency numbers to become subscripts for the opposite partner.
  4. Step 4: Simplify the ratio if both numbers can be divided (and drop the number 1).
Two Criss-Cross Walkthroughs
Example A: Aluminium Oxide
1. Symbols: \(\text{Al}\) and \(\text{O}\)
2. Valencies: \(\text{Al} = 3\), \(\text{O} = 2\)
3. Criss-cross the numbers: The 3 goes to Oxygen; the 2 goes to Aluminium.
4. Result: $$\mathbf{\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3}$$ Why it works: Two \(\text{Al}^{3+}\) ions provide \(2 \times (+3) = +6\). Three \(\text{O}^{2-}\) ions provide \(3 \times (-2) = -6\). The compound is perfectly neutral!
Example B: Calcium Hydroxide (Using Polyatomic Radicals)
1. Symbols: \(\text{Ca}\) and the Hydroxide group \(\text{OH}\)
2. Valencies: \(\text{Ca} = 2\), \(\text{OH} = 1\)
3. Criss-cross: The 2 goes to the entire \(\text{OH}\) group!
4. Notice the Brackets: Because \(\text{OH}\) is a team of atoms, we MUST enclose it in brackets before adding the subscript 2:
$$\mathbf{\text{Ca(OH)}_2}$$ Note on Polyatomic Radicals: If you write \(\text{CaOH}_2\), only the Hydrogen gets doubled! You must write \(\text{Ca(OH)}_2\).

6. Isotopes: The Atomic Twins

Imagine identical twin brothers. They have the exact same face, same voice, same DNA, and wear the exact same clothes. But one of them carries a heavy backpack full of books, so he weighs slightly more on a bathroom scale.

That is exactly what isotopes are.

What are Isotopes? Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have the exact same Atomic Number (\(Z\), same protons and electrons), but a different Mass Number (\(A\)) because they carry a different number of neutrons in their nucleus!
Hydrogen's Three Twins Notation Symbol Protons (\(p^+\)) Neutrons (\(n^0\)) Electrons (\(e^-\)) What's Special About It?
Protium \(_{1}^{1}\text{H}\) 1 0 1 Ordinary hydrogen (99.98% of all water). The only atom in the universe with zero neutrons!
Deuterium \(_{1}^{2}\text{H}\) (or \(\text{D}\)) 1 1 1 "Heavy hydrogen." Combines with oxygen to make Heavy Water (\(\text{D}_2\text{O}\)), used in nuclear power reactors.
Tritium \(_{1}^{3}\text{H}\) (or \(\text{T}\)) 1 2 1 Radioactive hydrogen. Unstable nucleus that glows in watch dials.
The Big Exam Question: Why Do Isotopes React Identically?

Chemical reactions are driven entirely by the outer shell electrons. The neutrons are locked away deep in the nucleus and take zero part in bonding.

Because all isotopes of an element have the exact same number of electrons, they have identical electronic configurations, meaning they exhibit identical chemical reactions! They only differ in physical weight-based properties like density or boiling points.

Worked Problems

Problem 2.1: Atomic Detective Work & Configuration ICSE Exam Standard • 4 Marks
An atom of element \(X\) has an atomic number of \(17\) and a mass number of \(35\).
(i) Identify the name and symbol of the element.
(ii) Calculate the exact number of protons, electrons, and neutrons.
(iii) Write down its electronic configuration across K, L, M shells.
(iv) State its valency and explain whether it behaves as a metal or non-metal.
(i) Element Identity:
Atomic number 17 is unique to Chlorine (\(\text{Cl}\)). [1 Mark]

(ii) Particle Counts:
• Number of protons (\(p\)) = Atomic Number (\(Z\)) = \(\mathbf{17}\)
• Number of electrons (\(e\)) = Protons (neutral atom) = \(\mathbf{17}\)
• Number of neutrons (\(n\)) = Mass Number (\(A\)) - Atomic Number (\(Z\)) = \(35 - 17 = \mathbf{18\text{ neutrons}}\). [1 Mark]

(iii) Electronic Configuration:
Arranging 17 electrons by filling shells from inside out:
• K shell takes maximum 2 electrons.
• L shell takes maximum 8 electrons.
• Remaining electrons = \(17 - (2 + 8) = 7\) go into M shell.
$$\text{Configuration} = \mathbf{2, 8, 7} \quad (\text{K=2, L=8, M=7})$$ [1 Mark]

(iv) Valency & Nature:
The outermost shell has 7 valence electrons. It needs 1 electron to complete its octet (8).
$$\text{Valency} = 8 - 7 = \mathbf{1}$$ Because it tends to gain an electron rather than lose electrons, it is a Non-Metal. [1 Mark]
Problem 2.2: Writing Chemical Formulas via Criss-Cross ICSE Exam Standard • 4 Marks
Using the valencies of the constituent elements and radicals, deduce the chemical formulas for:
(i) Magnesium Nitride (Magnesium valency = 2, Nitrogen valency = 3)
(ii) Aluminium Sulphate (Aluminium valency = 3, Sulphate radical \(\text{SO}_4\) valency = 2)
(i) Magnesium Nitride:
1. Write symbols: \(\text{Mg}\)     \(\text{N}\)
2. Write valencies above: \(\text{Mg}^{2}\)     \(\text{N}^{3}\)
3. Criss-cross the numbers: The 3 goes to Magnesium; the 2 goes to Nitrogen.
$$\text{Formula} = \mathbf{\text{Mg}_3\text{N}_2}$$ [2 Marks]

(ii) Aluminium Sulphate:
1. Write symbols: \(\text{Al}\)     \(\text{SO}_4\)
2. Write valencies above: \(\text{Al}^{3}\)     \((\text{SO}_4)^{2}\)
3. Criss-cross: The 2 goes to Aluminium; the 3 goes to the whole Sulphate radical.
4. Enclose the radical in brackets:
$$\text{Formula} = \mathbf{\text{Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3}$$ [2 Marks]
Problem 2.3: Isotope Comparison & Mass Discrepancies ICSE Exam Standard • 3 Marks
Naturally occurring Chlorine exists as two isotopes: \(_{17}^{35}\text{Cl}\) and \(_{17}^{37}\text{Cl}\).
(a) State the number of protons and neutrons in each isotope.
(b) Why do both isotopes show the exact same chemical reactions with Sodium to form table salt?
(a) Particle Counts:
• For \(_{17}^{35}\text{Cl}\): Protons = 17, Neutrons = \(35 - 17 = \mathbf{18\text{ neutrons}}\).
• For \(_{17}^{37}\text{Cl}\): Protons = 17, Neutrons = \(37 - 17 = \mathbf{20\text{ neutrons}}\). [1.5 Marks]

(b) Why Chemical Reactions are Identical:
Chemical reactivity is determined solely by the number and arrangement of valence electrons. Both isotopes have exactly 17 electrons arranged as **2, 8, 7**, meaning both need exactly 1 electron to complete their octet. Because their electronic configurations are identical, their chemical reactions with Sodium are 100% identical. [1.5 Marks]

Scientific Deductions & "Why?" Questions

Deduction 2.1: The Electrical Neutrality Paradox
Why doesn't a glass of water or a bar of iron give you an electric shock, even though it contains quadrillions of positively charged protons and negatively charged electrons?
Every neutral atom in the bar of iron contains the exact same number of protons as electrons. Because the positive electrical charge of a single proton (\(+1\)) precisely cancels the negative charge of a single electron (\(-1\)), the net electrical charge of every atom is identically zero. With quadrillions of positive and negative charges in perfect numerical balance, no net voltage or electrical force exists to deliver a shock.
Deduction 2.2: The Inertness of Noble Gases
Why do noble gases like Helium and Neon never rust, burn, or combine with other elements under ordinary conditions?
Chemical bonding only occurs when atoms need to achieve stability by emptying, filling, or sharing electrons in their outer shell. Helium already has 2 electrons in its only shell (a completely full duplet), and Neon has 8 electrons in its outer shell (a completely full octet). Because their valence shells are already completely full, their combining capacity (valency) is strictly zero. They have zero chemical motivation to give, take, or share electrons.

8. The Common-Sense Strategy: Reality Checks

Elimination Drill
"Common sense is the law." In atomic structure, formulas represent concrete subatomic particles. Top problem solvers don't blindly calculate; they inspect the options first and ask: Which of these violate fundamental shell limits or nucleon arithmetic? Use your physical instincts to eliminate wrong answers before writing down a single equation.
Check 01 The Octet Bound Rule

For Class 7 syllabus elements, valency is combining capacity and can never exceed 4 (\(0 \le \text{Valency} \le 4\)). Any option claiming a valency of 5, 6, or 7 is an immediate blunder.

Check 02 Subatomic Mass Conservation

Mass number \(A = Z + n\). Therefore, neutron count \(n = A - Z\). Neutrons can never exceed the mass number or be negative.

Check 03 The Shell Capacity Limit

By the \(2n^2\) rule, the K-shell (\(n=1\)) holds at most 2 electrons; the L-shell (\(n=2\)) holds at most 8 electrons. No shell can skip intermediate filling.

Interactive Elimination Challenge • Click Each Option to Test Target: Rule out 3 options in 10 seconds
Exam Problem: An atom of phosphorus has an atomic number \(Z = 15\) and a mass number \(A = 31\). What is its Bohr electronic configuration across shells \(K, L, M\) and its common chemical valency?
A Configuration: \(2, 8, 5\); Valency \(= 5\)
Click to test with common sense ⚡
⚡ Ruled Out in 2 Seconds Valence Electron Identity Confusion
Why common sense eliminates this: Valency is combining capacity (electrons gained, lost, or shared), NOT the count of outer electrons. Non-metals with 5 valence electrons do not lose 5 electrons; they gain \(8 - 5 = 3\) electrons. Valency never exceeds 4!
B Configuration: \(2, 13\); Valency \(= 3\)
Click to test with common sense ⚡
⚡ Ruled Out in 2 Seconds Shell Capacity Violation
Why common sense eliminates this: The L-shell has a principal quantum number \(n=2\). By the \(2n^2\) rule, its maximum holding capacity is \(2(2)^2 = 8\) electrons. Cramming 13 electrons into the second shell completely violates atomic physics!
C Configuration: \(2, 8, 8\); Valency \(= 0\)
Click to test with common sense ⚡
⚡ Ruled Out in 5 Seconds Argon Electron Count Confusion
Why common sense eliminates this: Summing the electrons gives \(2 + 8 + 8 = 18\) electrons, which belongs to inert Argon (\(Z=18\)). But phosphorus has only \(Z=15\) electrons!
D Configuration: \(2, 8, 5\); Valency \(= 3\)
Click to test with common sense ⚡
✓ The Only Survivor! Physical & Electronic Confirmation
Why common sense picks this: Total electrons \(= Z = 15\). Filled in order: K-shell \(= 2\), L-shell \(= 8\), remaining M-shell \(= 5\). Since it has 5 valence electrons, it requires 3 more electrons to complete its stable octet of 8: \(\text{Valency} = 8 - 5 = \mathbf{3}\).

The 10-Second Proof:
• Electronic Distribution: \(K(2), L(8), M(5) \implies 2 + 8 + 5 = 15 = Z\).
• Octet Rule: \(\text{Valency} = 8 - 5 = \mathbf{3}\).
