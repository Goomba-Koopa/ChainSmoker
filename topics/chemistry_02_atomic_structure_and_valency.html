Chemistry • Topic 02

Atomic Structure & Valency

Subatomic particles (proton, neutron, electron — mass, charge, location), atomic number (Z) and mass number (A), Bohr’s shell model with the 2n² rule, valency determination, and isotopes.

1. Subatomic Particles: The Building Blocks

Imagine a Lego castle built from tiny, identical plastic blocks. In the real world, everything around us—from the water we drink to the stars in the night sky—is constructed from incredibly tiny fundamental units called atoms. However, atoms are not indivisible spheres; they are complex structures made of even smaller components known as subatomic particles. The arrangement and quantity of these particles determine the entire chemical behavior of the universe.

Subatomic Particles: The fundamental constituent particles that make up an atom. The three primary subatomic particles are the proton (positively charged), the neutron (neutral or no charge), and the electron (negatively charged).

• Crucial Examiner Insight: Protons and neutrons are tightly bound together in the central core of the atom called the nucleus (collectively called nucleons), while electrons revolve around the nucleus in specific paths or orbits.
Particle Symbol Relative Charge Relative Mass (amu) Location in Atom
Proton \(p^+\) or \(p\) \(+1\) \(1 \text{ amu}\) Inside the central nucleus
Neutron \(n^0\) or \(n\) \(0\) (Neutral) \(1 \text{ amu}\) (slightly heavier than proton) Inside the central nucleus
Electron \(e^-\) \(-1\) \(\approx \frac{1}{1837} \text{ amu}\) (negligible) Revolving in extra-nuclear shells (orbits)

2. Atomic Number (Z) and Mass Number (A)

Just as every student in a classroom has a unique roll number that identifies them, every chemical element is uniquely identified by the number of protons residing in its nucleus. If you change the number of protons, you fundamentally change the element itself. Furthermore, since the nucleus contains the heavy particles (protons and neutrons), it represents practically the entire mass of the atom.

Atomic Number (\(Z\)): The total number of protons present in the nucleus of an atom. In a neutral atom, it is also exactly equal to the total number of revolving electrons.

Mass Number (\(A\)): The sum total of protons and neutrons (collectively called nucleons) present in the nucleus of an atom.
Fundamental Atomic Relationships
1. Atomic Number (\(Z\)) = Number of Protons (\(p\))
    In a neutral, uncharged atom: \(Z = p = e\)

2. Mass Number (\(A\)) = Number of Protons (\(p\)) + Number of Neutrons (\(n\))
    $$A = Z + n$$

3. Calculating Neutrons:
    To find the number of neutrons in any atom, simply subtract the atomic number from the mass number:
    $$n = A - Z$$

4. Standard Notation:
    An element \(X\) is represented as: $$_{Z}^{A}X$$

3. Bohr’s Shell Model & The \(2n^2\) Rule

Picture passengers boarding a bus with specific seating rows; they must fill the inner rows first before moving to the back. Similarly, electrons do not just randomly fly around the nucleus; they occupy specific, fixed circular paths called energy levels or shells. Niels Bohr proposed that these shells are filled in a strictly regulated sequence starting from the shell closest to the nucleus.

Bohr's Shell Model: Electrons revolve around the atomic nucleus in discrete, stable circular orbits called shells or energy levels, designated by the letters K, L, M, N... starting from the innermost shell.

The \(2n^2\) Rule (Bohr-Bury Scheme): The maximum number of electrons that can be accommodated in any given shell is given by the formula \(2n^2\), where \(n\) is the shell number (principal quantum number).
Shell Name Shell Number (\(n\)) Maximum Electrons Formula (\(2n^2\)) Maximum Capacity
K shell 1 \(2 \times (1)^2\) 2 electrons
L shell 2 \(2 \times (2)^2\) 8 electrons
M shell 3 \(2 \times (3)^2\) 18 electrons
N shell 4 \(2 \times (4)^2\) 32 electrons

4. Valency Determination

Consider a puzzle piece that needs exactly two complementary hooks to attach to another piece seamlessly. Atoms behave similarly—they interact and combine with other atoms to achieve a highly stable, "complete" outermost shell (usually 8 electrons, called an octet). Valency measures this combining capacity, dictating how many electrons an atom must give away, steal, or share to reach perfectly stable serenity.

Valency: The combining capacity of an element. It is defined as the number of electrons an atom of an element loses, gains, or shares during a chemical reaction to attain a stable noble gas electronic configuration (an octet or duplet).

Valence Electrons: The electrons present in the outermost shell of an atom. These are the only electrons that participate in chemical bonding.
Rules for Determining Valency
1. Write down the electronic configuration of the element.
2. Note the number of valence electrons (electrons in the outermost shell).
3. For metals (1, 2, or 3 valence electrons):
    They tend to lose electrons to become stable.
    \(\text{Valency} = \text{Number of valence electrons}\)
    Example: Magnesium (\(2, 8, 2\)) has 2 valence e⁻. It loses 2 e⁻. Valency = 2.
4. For non-metals (5, 6, or 7 valence electrons):
    They tend to gain electrons to complete their octet (8).
    \(\text{Valency} = 8 - \text{Number of valence electrons}\)
    Example: Oxygen (\(2, 6\)) has 6 valence e⁻. It needs 2 e⁻ to reach 8. Valency = 2.
5. For elements with 4 valence electrons (like Carbon):
    They typically share 4 electrons. Valency = 4.
6. For noble gases (8 valence electrons; 2 for Helium):
    Their outermost shell is perfectly full. They do not need to lose or gain. Valency = 0.

5. Isotopes: Variations of an Element

Think of identical twins. They look the same, act the same, and have the same DNA, but one might weigh slightly more than the other because they ate more for dinner. Isotopes are the chemical equivalent of twins. They are atoms of the exact same element (same protons) that exhibit identical chemical behaviors but happen to carry a few extra neutrons, making them physically heavier.

Isotopes: Atoms of the same element having the same atomic number (\(Z\)) but a different mass number (\(A\)). This difference in mass is entirely due to a different number of neutrons in their respective nuclei.

• Crucial Examiner Insight: Because isotopes have the same number of protons, they also have the same number of electrons, resulting in an identical electronic configuration. Thus, isotopes exhibit identical chemical properties, but slightly different physical properties (like density or boiling point) due to mass variation.
Isotope of Hydrogen Symbol Notation Protons (\(p\)) Neutrons (\(n\)) Electrons (\(e^-\))
Protium (Ordinary Hydrogen) \(_{1}^{1}\text{H}\) 1 0 1
Deuterium (Heavy Hydrogen) \(_{1}^{2}\text{H}\) or \(\text{D}\) 1 1 1
Tritium (Radioactive Hydrogen) \(_{1}^{3}\text{H}\) or \(\text{T}\) 1 2 1

6. Worked Problems

Problem 2.1: Atomic Identification & Bohr Diagram [4 Marks]
An element X has atomic number 17 and mass number 35.
(i) Name the element.
(ii) Draw its Bohr diagram.
(iii) State its valency.
(iv) How many neutrons does it have?
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. (i) Name the element:
    The element with atomic number 17 (\(Z=17\)) is Chlorine (Cl). [1 Mark]

2. (ii) Draw its Bohr diagram:
    Number of electrons = Atomic number = 17.
    Electronic configuration: 2, 8, 7 (K=2, L=8, M=7).
    (To draw, the student must draw a central nucleus marked with '17p', surrounded by 3 concentric circles. Plot 2 dots on the first circle, 8 on the second, and 7 on the outermost circle.) [1 Mark]

3. (iii) State its valency:
    Outermost shell has 7 electrons. It needs 1 more electron to complete its octet.
    Valency = \(8 - 7 = \mathbf{1}\). [1 Mark]

4. (iv) How many neutrons does it have?
    Number of neutrons (\(n\)) = Mass number (\(A\)) - Atomic number (\(Z\))
    \(n = 35 - 17 = \mathbf{18\text{ neutrons}}\). [1 Mark]
Problem 2.2: Isotope Analysis [3 Marks]
Deuterium (atomic mass 2) and Protium (atomic mass 1) are isotopes of Hydrogen. Explain why they are isotopes and state one similarity and one difference in their properties.
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. Why they are isotopes:
    Both Deuterium and Protium belong to the same element (Hydrogen) because they have the exact same atomic number (1 proton). However, they have different mass numbers (2 and 1) due to a differing number of neutrons (Deuterium has 1 neutron, Protium has 0). [1.5 Marks]

2. Similarity in properties:
    Because they have the identical number of electrons (1), their electronic configurations are identical. Therefore, they have identical chemical properties. [0.75 Marks]

3. Difference in properties:
    Because Deuterium has a neutron, it is heavier. Therefore, they exhibit different physical properties (such as Deuterium forming 'heavy water' which has a higher boiling point than normal water). [0.75 Marks]

7. Give Scientific Reasons

Reasoning Question 1
Why is an atom electrically neutral despite containing positively charged protons and negatively charged electrons?
Model Examiner Answer:
An atom is electrically neutral because the number of positively charged protons located in the nucleus is exactly equal to the number of negatively charged electrons revolving in the orbits. The total positive charge precisely cancels out the total negative charge, resulting in a net electrical charge of zero.
Reasoning Question 2
Why do noble gases like Helium and Neon exhibit zero valency (inertness)?
Model Examiner Answer:
Noble gases have completely filled outermost electron shells. Helium has 2 electrons in its only shell (a stable duplet), and Neon has 8 electrons in its outermost shell (a stable octet). Because their valence shells are perfectly full according to the \(2n^2\) rule, they neither need to lose, gain, nor share electrons to achieve stability, making their combining capacity (valency) zero.
