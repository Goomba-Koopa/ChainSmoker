Physics • Topic 04

Sound: Waves, Propagation & Properties

Longitudinal wave nature of sound, the bell-jar vacuum experiment, speed of sound in solids/liquids/gases, pitch vs loudness vs quality, and the audible frequency range (20 Hz–20,000 Hz).

Learning Goals Skills you will master in this lesson
  • ✓
    Demonstrate Medium Requirement via Bell-Jar: Prove experimentally why mechanical sound waves cannot propagate through a vacuum (\(v = 0\text{ m/s}\)).
  • ✓
    Compare Propagation Speeds Across Media: Explain why sound travels fastest in solids and slowest in gases based on elastic particle packing density (\(v_{\text{solid}} > v_{\text{liquid}} > v_{\text{gas}}\)).
  • ✓
    Distinguish Loudness, Pitch, and Quality: Correlate wave amplitude to loudness, frequency to pitch (shrillness), and waveform complexity to timbre (quality).
  • ✓

The Grade 6 Bridge

From Grade 6 Intuition to Grade 7 Reality

Physics builds layer upon layer. Here is how what you learned in Grade 6 evolves into Grade 7 acoustics:
Grade 6: The Simple Model Sensory hearing and vibrating objects
  • Sound comes from vibrations: You observed vibrating rulers on table edges, plucked rubber bands, and vibrating tuning forks.
  • Sound travels through air to ears: Sound was described as a sensation traveling across space without analyzing pressure compressions or wave mechanics.
  • Sounds were loud or soft: Differences were observed qualitatively without distinguishing between amplitude (volume) and frequency (pitch).
Grade 7: The Deeper Reality Mechanical longitudinal waves and acoustic boundaries
  • Sound is a mechanical longitudinal wave: Air particles oscillate parallel to wave motion, forming alternating compressions and rarefactions; sound cannot travel through a vacuum.
  • Speed depends on elasticity and density: Sound travels at \(\sim 5000\text{ m/s}\) in steel, \(\sim 1500\text{ m/s}\) in water, and \(\sim 330\text{--}340\text{ m/s}\) in air at \(0^\circ\text{C}\).
  • Quantitative frequency & audibility: Frequency is calculated in Hertz (\(\text{Hz} = \text{vibrations/second}\)), with human hearing strictly bounded between \(20\text{ Hz}\) and \(20,000\text{ Hz}\).

1. The Longitudinal Wave Nature of Sound

When a large drum is struck, you can often feel the floor or the air near it vibrate as the sound wave compresses the air towards you. These vibrations travel outward by pushing and pulling the particles of the air back and forth. Because the particles vibrate in the exact same direction as the wave is moving, this creates alternating regions of high pressure and low pressure.

Longitudinal Wave: A wave in which the particles of the medium vibrate back and forth along the same direction (parallel) to the direction of propagation of the wave.

• Crucial Examiner Insight: A sound wave consists of compressions (regions where particles are crowded together and pressure is high) and rarefactions (regions where particles are spread apart and pressure is low).

2. The Bell-Jar Vacuum Experiment

Sound needs a material medium to travel; if you were in outer space, you wouldn't be able to hear a friend screaming right next to you because there is no air. The famous Bell-Jar experiment proves that sound cannot propagate through a vacuum.

Material Medium Requirement: Sound waves are mechanical waves that require a material medium (solid, liquid, or gas) for their propagation. They cannot travel through a vacuum.

• Bell-Jar Experiment: An electric bell is suspended inside an airtight glass bell jar connected to a vacuum pump. When the bell rings with air inside, the sound is heard clearly. As the vacuum pump removes the air, the sound becomes fainter and fainter until it is completely inaudible, even though the hammer is still seen striking the gong.

3. Speed of Sound in Solids, Liquids, and Gases

If you press your ear to a steel train track, you can hear an approaching train long before you hear its sound through the air. This is because sound travels much faster through rigid solids than through easily compressible gases. The closer the particles are packed in a medium, the faster they can transfer the vibrational energy to their neighbors.

Medium State Approximate Speed of Sound Reason for Speed Variation Example
Gas \(330\text{ to }340\text{ m/s}\) (at room temp) Particles are far apart and loosely bound, so transferring vibrations takes more time. Air
Liquid \(\sim 1,500\text{ m/s}\) Particles are closer together than in gases, allowing faster transmission. Water
Solid \(5,000\text{ to }6,000\text{ m/s}\) Particles are tightly packed and strongly bound, instantly transferring vibrational energy. Steel, Iron
Vacuum \(0\text{ m/s}\) No particles exist to transfer the mechanical vibrations. Outer Space

4. Pitch, Loudness, and Quality

A roaring lion produces a very loud sound with a low pitch, while a mosquito's buzz is quiet but has a high, irritating pitch. Two different instruments, like a guitar and a flute, can play the exact same loud, high-pitched note, yet you can easily tell them apart. These differences are defined by three distinct characteristics of sound.

Characteristic Depends On Description Unit / Measurement
Loudness Amplitude Distinguishes a loud sound from a faint one. Higher amplitude creates louder sound. Decibel (dB)
Pitch Frequency Distinguishes a shrill (high) sound from a flat (low) sound. Higher frequency = higher pitch. Hertz (Hz)
Quality (Timbre) Waveform Distinguishes two sounds of the same loudness and pitch from different sources. N/A

5. The Audible Frequency Range

Special dog whistles emit sounds at frequencies above 20,000 vibrations per second—so high that human ears detect absolutely nothing, but dogs perceive a loud, sharp screech. The human ear has a limited window of frequencies it can detect.

Audible Range: The range of frequencies of sound that a normal human ear can hear, which is from 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz.

• Infrasonic sound: Frequencies below 20 Hz (e.g., sounds produced by earthquakes or elephants).
• Ultrasonic sound: Frequencies above 20,000 Hz (e.g., sounds produced by bats, dolphins, and dog whistles).

6. Worked Problems

Problem 4.1: Speed Calculation [3 Marks]
A sound wave travels \(1,700\text{ m}\) in \(5\text{ seconds}\) in air. Calculate its speed. Is this faster or slower than in water (\(1500\text{ m/s}\))?
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. Identify Given Values:
    Distance (\(d\)) = \(1,700\text{ m}\)
    Time (\(t\)) = \(5\text{ s}\)

2. Formula and Calculation:
    $$\text{Speed } (v) = \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Time}}$$ [1 Mark]
    $$v = \frac{1700\text{ m}}{5\text{ s}} = \mathbf{340\text{ m/s}}$$ [1 Mark]

3. Comparison:
    The speed in air (\(340\text{ m/s}\)) is much slower than the speed in water (\(1500\text{ m/s}\)). [1 Mark]
Problem 4.2: Echo Distance Calculation [3 Marks]
A boy shouts near a cliff and hears the echo after \(3\text{ seconds}\). If the speed of sound is \(340\text{ m/s}\), how far is the cliff?
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. Identify Given Values:
    Total time for echo (\(t\)) = \(3\text{ s}\)
    Speed of sound (\(v\)) = \(340\text{ m/s}\)

2. Understand the Concept:
    An echo is the reflection of sound. The sound travels to the cliff and back, covering twice the distance (\(2d\)).
    $$2d = v \times t$$ [1 Mark]

3. Calculation:
    $$2d = 340\text{ m/s} \times 3\text{ s} = 1020\text{ m}$$ [1 Mark]
    $$d = \frac{1020}{2} = \mathbf{510\text{ m}}$$ [1 Mark]
    The cliff is \(510\text{ m}\) away.

7. Give Scientific Reasons

Reasoning Item
Q: Why do we see the lightning flash before we hear the thunder, even though they occur simultaneously?
Model Examiner Answer:
The speed of light in air is exceptionally high (\(3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}\)), while the speed of sound in air is comparatively very low (\(\sim 340\text{ m/s}\)). Therefore, the light from the flash reaches our eyes almost instantaneously, whereas the sound of thunder takes significantly more time to travel the same distance.
Reasoning Item
Q: Why can bats navigate in complete darkness without colliding with obstacles?
Model Examiner Answer:
Bats emit high-frequency ultrasonic squeaks (above 20,000 Hz) that travel through the air. When these ultrasonic waves hit an obstacle or prey, they reflect back as an echo. By hearing the echo, the bat determines the distance, direction, and nature of the obstacle, a process known as echolocation.
Exam Strategy • The 5-Second Filter

8. The Common-Sense Strategy: Rule Out the Impossible

Elimination Drill
"Common sense is the law." In science and mathematics, formulas are not abstract numbers—they govern physical reality. Top problem solvers don't blindly grind algebra; they inspect options first and ask: Which of these are physically absurd or mathematically impossible? In multiple-choice questions, you can frequently rule out 2 or 3 options in under 5 seconds.
Check 01 The Medium Requirement Check

Mechanical sound waves strictly require a material medium (solid, liquid, or gas) to propagate. Sound cannot travel through empty space or a vacuum (\(v = 0\text{ m/s}\)).

Check 02 The Velocity Order Invariant

Sound speeds strictly follow elastic density: \(v_{\text{solid}} > v_{\text{liquid}} > v_{\text{gas}}\). Sound travels at \(\sim 5000\text{ m/s}\) in steel, \(\sim 1500\text{ m/s}\) in water, and only \(\sim 340\text{ m/s}\) in air.

Check 03 The Human Hearing Bracket

Human ears can only detect vibrational frequencies between \(20\text{ Hz}\) and \(20,000\text{ Hz}\). Frequencies below \(20\text{ Hz}\) are infrasonic; frequencies above \(20\text{ kHz}\) are ultrasonic.

9. The Bug Hunt: Audit Rohan's Homework

Interactive Audit • 3 Marks
Problem Assigned:
A laboratory tuning fork completes 600 complete oscillations in \(2.5\text{ seconds}\). Calculate its frequency in Hertz (\(\text{Hz}\)) and state whether the sound produced is audible to human ears.
Class 7 student Rohan submitted the three-step solution below in his exercise book. Click on any line to put on the examiner's red pen: verify whether the step is sound, catch root blunders, or see how ICSE awards follow-through credit (ECF).
Student: Rohan Sharma • Class 7-B Physics Assignment: Sound Waves & Frequency
Step 1: Given Data & Target Identification Click to inspect line 🔍
Given: Number of oscillations \(N = 600\), Time elapsed \(t = 2.5\text{ s}\). Target: Frequency \(f\) and human audibility.
Step 2: Frequency Calculation Click to inspect line 🔍
$$f = \frac{t}{N} = \frac{2.5\text{ s}}{600} = 0.00417\text{ Hz}$$
Step 3: Audibility Deduction Click to inspect line 🔍
Since \(0.00417\text{ Hz} < 20\text{ Hz}\), the sound is infrasonic and completely inaudible to humans.
✓ Sound Step Score: 1/1 Mark
Examiner's Assessment: Correct Extraction of Given Parameters
Rohan accurately extracted the number of oscillations (\(N = 600\)) and elapsed time (\(t = 2.5\text{ s}\)), setting up the calculation clearly. No deduction here!
✗ Root Blunder Caught! Deduction: -1 Mark
Examiner's Assessment: Inverted Frequency Formula (\(t/N\) instead of \(N/t\))
The Trap: Rohan inverted the definition of frequency! Frequency is the number of oscillations per second (\(f = \frac{N}{t}\)).
• Dividing time by oscillations (\(\frac{t}{N}\)) calculates the Time Period (\(T = 0.00417\text{ s}\)), not frequency.
• The correct frequency is \(f = \frac{600}{2.5\text{ s}} = \mathbf{240\text{ Hz}}\).
🟡 Follow-Through (ECF Credit) Method Credit: 1/1 Mark (M1)
Examiner's Assessment: Error Carried Forward (ECF)
How ICSE Marks This: Rohan correctly remembered that sounds below \(20\text{ Hz}\) are infrasonic and inaudible to human ears.
Because his audibility deduction followed strictly from his (erroneous) carried value of \(0.00417\text{ Hz}\), ICSE examiners award the Method Mark (M1) to prevent double penalization.
Examiner's Corrected Model Answer:

1. Recall the Frequency Definition Formula:
    $$f = \frac{\text{Total Number of Vibrations } (N)}{\text{Time taken in seconds } (t)}$$
2. Calculate Frequency in Hertz:
    $$f = \frac{600}{2.5\text{ s}} = \mathbf{240\text{ Hz}}$$
    Common-sense reality check: A laboratory tuning fork produces a clear audible hum around musical note B3/Middle C. It vibrates hundreds of times each second, not once every four minutes!

3. Determine Human Audibility:
    The normal human hearing range extends from \(20\text{ Hz}\) to \(20,000\text{ Hz}\). Because \(240\text{ Hz}\) falls within this range, the sound is **clearly audible to human ears**.
