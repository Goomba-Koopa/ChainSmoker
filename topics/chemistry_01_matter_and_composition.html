The Grade 6 Bridge

From Grade 6 Intuition to Grade 7 Reality

Chemistry builds layer upon layer. Here is how what you learned in Grade 6 evolves into Grade 7 molecular rigor:
Grade 6: The Simple Model Macroscopic observation and shape descriptions
  • States were about shapes: Solids hold their shape, liquids take the shape of the container, and gases spread to fill any closed container.
  • Changes were hot or cold: Ice melts when warmed; water boils when heated over a stove. Heating was thought to always raise temperature.
  • Substances were continuous: Water seemed like a continuous smooth fluid rather than billions of discrete particles in violent kinetic motion.
Grade 7: The Deeper Reality Kinetic molecular mechanics and latent heat
  • The Intermolecular Tug-of-War: State of matter is determined by the balance between cohesive intermolecular attractive forces and the kinetic energy (\(KE \propto T\)) of vibrating particles.
  • Intermolecular Spaces & Forces: Solids have minimal spaces and maximum forces; gases have vast spaces (\(\sim 10\times\) molecular diameter) and negligible attraction, explaining high compressibility.
  • Latent Heat Breaks Bonds: During melting or boiling, supplied thermal energy does not increase particle velocity; it is consumed as latent heat to overcome attractive bonds at fixed plateau temperatures.

1. The Kinetic Molecular Theory of Matter

If someone sprays perfume on one side of a room, you can soon smell it on the other side. This happens because all matter is made of tiny, invisible particles that are constantly moving. The Kinetic Molecular Theory explains how these particles behave, depending on how much energy they have.

Kinetic Molecular Theory (Postulates): The fundamental scientific model stating that:
1. All matter is composed of extremely small particles (atoms, molecules, or ions).
2. These particles are in a state of continuous random motion, and thus possess kinetic energy.
3. The kinetic energy of the particles increases with an increase in absolute temperature.
4. There are empty spaces between these particles, known as intermolecular spaces.
5. There exists a force of attraction between the particles, called intermolecular force, which keeps them together.

2. Intermolecular Forces vs. Intermolecular Spaces

You cannot easily compress a wooden block, but you can effortlessly push the plunger of an empty syringe with your thumb blocking the tip. The wood resists because its particles are tightly packed with very strong attractive forces holding them in place. The air inside the syringe has large spaces between its particles and weak attractive forces, allowing them to be pushed closer together.

Intermolecular Force of Attraction: The physical force of attraction existing between the adjacent molecules of a substance. It is maximum in solids, intermediate in liquids, and negligible in gases.

Intermolecular Space: The empty gap or distance between the individual molecules of a given state of matter. It is inversely proportional to the intermolecular force.

3. The Three Fundamental States of Matter

Water is unique because we commonly see it as solid ice, liquid water, and gaseous steam. The state depends entirely on the balance between intermolecular spaces and forces at a given temperature.

Property Solids Liquids Gases
Shape and Volume Definite shape and definite volume. Indefinite shape (takes the shape of the container) but definite volume. Indefinite shape and indefinite volume.
Intermolecular Space Negligible (particles are closely packed). Larger than in solids but smaller than in gases. Very large (particles are far apart).
Intermolecular Force Maximum (very strong). Intermediate (weaker than in solids). Negligible or extremely weak.
Compressibility Highly incompressible. Slightly compressible. Highly compressible.
Particle Motion Particles only vibrate about their mean positions. Particles can slip and slide over one another. Particles move randomly in all possible directions at high speeds.

4. Brownian Motion & Diffusion

When you look closely at dust motes dancing chaotically in a bright sunbeam, you are witnessing the effects of invisible air molecules constantly bombarding the dust. Similarly, dropping a single drop of red food coloring into a glass of still water eventually turns the entire glass red without any stirring. These phenomena provide direct physical evidence for the kinetic theory.

Brownian Motion: The continuous, random, zig-zag motion of microscopic particles suspended in a fluid (a liquid or a gas), caused by the uneven bombardment of the particles by the fast-moving molecules of the surrounding fluid.

Diffusion: The spontaneous mixing and intermingling of particles of two or more different substances on their own, moving from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, until a uniform mixture is formed.

5. Changes of State & Latent Heat

When you put an ice cube in a warm drink, it begins to melt. Interestingly, a thermometer placed in the melting ice will read exactly 0°C until the very last piece of ice has melted, even though heat is constantly entering it. This "hidden" heat is not raising the temperature; it is being used to break the strong intermolecular forces holding the solid water molecules together.

Change of State: The physical process of a substance transforming from one state of matter to another without any change in its chemical composition, usually achieved by changing the temperature or pressure. Examples include melting (solid to liquid), boiling (liquid to gas), and sublimation (solid directly to gas).

Latent Heat: The amount of heat energy absorbed or released by a substance during a change of state, which occurs at a constant temperature. It is termed 'latent' (meaning hidden) because it does not register as a temperature change on a thermometer.

6. CISCE Worked Problem Problems & Give Scientific Reasons

Question 1: Give Scientific Reasons
(a) Why does a gas fill the entire container it is placed in?
(b) Why does a solid maintain its own shape?
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. Part (a): Gas filling the container
    In a gas, the intermolecular forces of attraction are almost negligible. [1 Mark]
    As a result, the gas particles are completely free to move randomly in all directions at high speeds. They continuously expand to occupy all available intermolecular space, thus completely filling whatever container they are placed in. [1 Mark]

2. Part (b): Solid maintaining shape
    In a solid, the intermolecular forces of attraction are extremely strong and intermolecular spaces are negligible. [1 Mark]
    Because of this, the particles are held rigidly in fixed positions. They can only vibrate about their mean positions and cannot slide past one another, giving the solid a definite, rigid shape. [1 Mark]
Question 2: Distinction
Distinguish between evaporation and boiling. State two differences.
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. Difference 1 (Temperature Dependence):
    Evaporation takes place at all temperatures (below the boiling point), whereas boiling takes place only at a specific, fixed temperature called the boiling point. [1 Mark]

2. Difference 2 (Site of occurrence):
    Evaporation is a slow surface phenomenon (it only happens at the exposed surface of the liquid), whereas boiling is a rapid bulk phenomenon (bubbles form throughout the entire volume of the liquid). [1 Mark]

5. Worked Problems

Problem 1.1: Kinetic Particle Velocity & Thermal Expansion [3 Marks]
A sealed copper sphere contains 0.5 kg of air at 25°C. When heated to 100°C: (i) What happens to the kinetic energy of the air particles? (ii) What happens to the internal pressure? (iii) Does the mass of air change?
Step-by-Step Solution:

1. Part (i): Kinetic Energy: Increases proportionally with absolute temperature. Particles vibrate and move at higher velocities.

2. Part (ii): Internal Pressure: Increases because faster gas molecules collide more frequently and violently against the sphere's inner walls.

3. Part (iii): Mass: Remains strictly constant (0.5 kg) according to the Law of Conservation of Mass.
Problem 1.2: Latent Heat of Fusion [2 Marks]
Why does the temperature of melting ice remain constant at 0°C even though heat is continuously being supplied by a flame?
Step-by-Step Solution:

The continuous thermal energy supplied does not increase particle kinetic energy; instead, it is consumed as Latent Heat of Fusion to overcome strong intermolecular forces of attraction holding the ice crystal lattice together. Until the entire solid transitions into liquid, the temperature remains fixed at 0°C.
Exam Strategy • The 5-Second Filter

8. The Common-Sense Strategy: Reality Checks

Elimination Drill
"Common sense is the law." In chemistry, formulas describe tangible physical reality. Top problem solvers don't blindly calculate; they inspect the options first and ask: Which of these are physically absurd or violate kinetic principles? Use your physical instincts to eliminate wrong answers before writing down a single equation.
Check 01 The Kinetic Energy-Temperature Rule

Temperature is a direct measure of average molecular kinetic energy (\(KE \propto T\)). If temperature is constant, particle kinetic energy cannot change, regardless of heat input.

Check 02 The Latent Heat Plateau Rule

During any phase change (melting at \(0^\circ\text{C}\), boiling at \(100^\circ\text{C}\)), temperature remains completely flat until all bonds between molecules are severed.

Check 03 The Incompressibility of Condensed States

Solids and liquids already have closely packed molecules with virtually zero empty space. Applying ordinary pressure cannot significantly alter their volume.

✓ The Only Survivor! Physical & Thermodynamic Confirmation
Why common sense picks this: Temperature strictly measures the average kinetic energy of molecules. The 10-Second Proof:
• Heat input during phase transition = \(\Delta Q = m \cdot L_f\).
• \(\Delta KE = 0 \implies \Delta T = 0\). Temperature remains strictly pinned at \(0^\circ\text{C}\).

✗ Root Blunder Caught! Deduction: -1 Mark
Examiner's Assessment: Violation of Latent Heat Plateau Rule
The Trap: Meera assumed that continuous heating must always cause temperature to rise.
• During boiling, liquid water and steam coexist. All heat supplied by the flame is consumed as Latent Heat of Vaporization (\(2260\text{ J/g}\)) to break liquid intermolecular bonds.
• Therefore, the temperature of boiling water remains strictly fixed at \(100^\circ\text{C}\) until every drop has converted into steam. Liquid water cannot reach \(103^\circ\text{C}\) or \(112^\circ\text{C}\) in an open container!

Examiner's Corrected Model Answer:

1. State the Boiling Equilibrium Principle:
    At standard atmospheric pressure (\(1\text{ atm}\)), pure liquid water transitions to steam at its fixed boiling point of \(100^\circ\text{C}\).

2. Apply Latent Heat of Vaporization:
    $$\text{Temperature during phase transition} = 100^\circ\text{C} \quad (\text{constant})$$
    Common-sense reality check: Additional heat from a high flame increases the rate of vaporization (more bubbles and steam per second), but cannot increase the temperature of liquid water in an open vessel.

3. Expected Readings & Average:
    $$\text{Readings: } 100^\circ\text{C}, 100^\circ\text{C}, 100^\circ\text{C}, 100^\circ\text{C} \implies \text{Average} = \mathbf{100^\circ\text{C}}$$
