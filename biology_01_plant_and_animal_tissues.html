Biology • Topic 01

Plant & Animal Tissues

Meristematic vs Permanent plant tissues (Simple: Parenchyma/Collenchyma/Sclerenchyma; Complex: Xylem/Phloem), and the four animal tissue systems (Epithelial, Connective, Muscular, Nervous) with functions.

1. Plant Tissues: Meristematic and Permanent

Just like how human fingernails and hair grow from specific regions, plants also grow from dedicated zones. If you observe a growing plant, its height increases mainly at the tips of the roots and stems. The tissue responsible for this active growth is meristematic tissue, characterized by rapid cell division. As these cells mature, they take on specialized roles and lose their ability to divide, becoming permanent tissue, much like how a student graduates to become a specialized professional like a doctor or engineer.

Meristematic & Permanent Tissues: Meristematic tissue is a group of actively dividing cells located at the growing points of a plant (like root and shoot tips) responsible for the increase in length and girth. Permanent tissue consists of mature, differentiated cells that have lost the power of division and are adapted to perform specific functions like protection, support, or transport.
Feature Meristematic Tissue Permanent Tissue
Cell Division Cells divide actively and continuously. Cells have lost the ability to divide.
Vacuoles Absent or very small (cytoplasm is dense). Large central vacuole is usually present.
Cell Wall Very thin and cellulosic. Often thick; may be lignified or suberized.
Intercellular Spaces Absent; cells are compactly arranged. Often present (especially in parenchyma).

2. Simple Permanent Tissues

Imagine building a house using only one type of brick. Simple permanent tissues are similar; they are composed of only one type of cell working together. If you bite into a soft potato, you are eating parenchyma cells which store food. When a strong wind blows and a young plant stem bends without breaking, it is due to collenchyma providing flexibility. The hard, crunchy grit in a pear or the tough shell of a coconut is made of dead, thickened cells called sclerenchyma.

Simple Permanent Tissues: Tissues made up of only one uniform type of cells that have a common origin and perform a similar function. They are primarily responsible for support and storage. They are categorized into three main types: Parenchyma, Collenchyma, and Sclerenchyma.
Tissue Type Nature of Cells Cell Wall Thickening Primary Function
Parenchyma Living cells with large vacuoles. Thin cell walls, usually made of cellulose. Storage of food and water; photosynthesis (if chloroplasts are present).
Collenchyma Living cells, often elongated. Unevenly thickened at the corners (pectin). Provides mechanical support and flexibility to young stems and leaves.
Sclerenchyma Dead cells at maturity, empty lumen. Uniformly highly thickened with lignin. Provides intense structural rigidity, mechanical strength, and protection.

3. Complex Permanent Tissues: Xylem and Phloem

A tall tree requires a sophisticated plumbing system to transport water from the soil up to the highest leaves, and to distribute the sugary food made in the leaves to the rest of the plant. Complex permanent tissues serve this exact purpose. They are like a network of pipes. Xylem pulls water upwards against gravity, acting like the water supply pipes in a building. Phloem distributes the prepared food in various directions, acting like a delivery network.

Complex Permanent Tissues (Vascular Tissues): Tissues composed of more than one type of cell that coordinate to perform a common complex function, primarily the transport of water, minerals, and nutrients throughout the plant body. The two types are Xylem and Phloem.
Characteristic Xylem Phloem
Direction of Transport Unidirectional (upwards from roots to leaves). Bidirectional (upwards and downwards from leaves).
Material Transported Water and dissolved minerals. Synthesized food (sugars/sucrose) and amino acids.
Nature of Cells Mostly dead cells at maturity (except xylem parenchyma). Mostly living cells (except phloem fibres).
Key Components Tracheids, vessels, xylem fibres, xylem parenchyma. Sieve tubes, companion cells, phloem parenchyma, phloem fibres.

4. The Four Animal Tissue Systems

Unlike plants, animals are highly mobile and have complex organ systems. An animal's body is built from just four basic types of building materials (tissues). Your skin, acting as a waterproof shield, is epithelial tissue. Your blood, bones, and tendons which bind everything together form connective tissue. The biceps you use to lift a bag rely on muscular tissue. Finally, when you touch something hot and instantly pull your hand away, it's the fast-acting electrical signals of the nervous tissue keeping you safe.

The Four Basic Animal Tissues: 1. Epithelial Tissue: Protective covering tissue covering external body surfaces and lining internal organs.
2. Connective Tissue: Tissue that binds, supports, and connects different organs (e.g., blood, bone, cartilage).
3. Muscular Tissue: Tissue composed of contractile muscle fibers responsible for movement.
4. Nervous Tissue: Tissue specialized for receiving stimuli and transmitting electrical impulses (composed of neurons).
Tissue System Primary Function Key Example Locations Special Characteristics
Epithelial Protection, absorption, secretion, and sensation. Skin surface, lining of mouth, intestines, blood vessels. Cells tightly packed with almost no intercellular spaces. Rests on a basement membrane.
Connective Binding, structural support, transport, and immunity. Bones, cartilage, tendons, ligaments, blood, adipose (fat). Cells are scattered within a rich extracellular matrix (solid, jelly-like, or fluid).
Muscular Voluntary and involuntary movements of the body. Heart (cardiac), attached to bones (skeletal), stomach walls (smooth). Contains special contractile proteins that allow them to shorten and relax.
Nervous Control and coordination via electrical nerve impulses. Brain, spinal cord, peripheral nerves. Composed of neurons with long axons; highly excitable, but do not divide.

5. Worked CISCE Exam Problems

Question 1: Distinction Question [5 Marks]
Distinguish between Xylem and Phloem under the following headings:
(i) Type of cells
(ii) Direction of transport
(iii) Material transported
(iv) Presence of living cells
(v) Location in stem
Examiner Solution:

(i) Type of cells: Xylem consists mainly of dead cells (tracheids, vessels, fibres) with only xylem parenchyma being living. Phloem consists mostly of living cells (sieve tubes, companion cells, parenchyma) with only phloem fibres being dead. [1 Mark]

(ii) Direction of transport: Xylem transports materials strictly upwards (unidirectional) from roots to leaves. Phloem transports materials both upwards and downwards (bidirectional) from leaves to other parts. [1 Mark]

(iii) Material transported: Xylem transports water and dissolved mineral salts. Phloem transports synthesized food (organic solutes like sucrose). [1 Mark]

(iv) Presence of living cells: Xylem has very few living cells (only parenchyma). Phloem is primarily composed of living cells. [1 Mark]

(v) Location in stem: In a typical stem vascular bundle, xylem is located on the inner side (towards the pith), whereas phloem is located on the outer side (towards the cortex). [1 Mark]
Question 2: Give Scientific Reasons [3 Marks]
Give Scientific Reasons for the following statements:
(i) Meristematic cells have no vacuoles.
(ii) Sclerenchyma cells are dead at maturity.
(iii) Tendons are made of white fibrous connective tissue.
Examiner Solution:

(i) Meristematic cells have no vacuoles: The primary function of meristematic cells is rapid cell division, not food or waste storage. Large vacuoles would hinder rapid division and take up space needed for a dense, highly active cytoplasm. [1 Mark]

(ii) Sclerenchyma cells are dead at maturity: Sclerenchyma cells develop heavily thickened walls deposited with a waterproof substance called lignin. This extreme lignification cuts off the exchange of materials with the environment, causing the protoplasm to die, leaving a dead structural cell for immense mechanical strength. [1 Mark]

(iii) Tendons are made of white fibrous connective tissue: Tendons connect muscles to bones and must withstand extreme tension during muscle contraction. White fibrous connective tissue is rich in collagen fibres, providing the very high tensile strength and tough, non-elastic nature required for this mechanical role. [1 Mark]

6. Give Scientific Reasons

Histology 1: Meristematic Tissue Cell Characteristics
Why do meristematic cells lack large vacuoles and possess thin cellulose cell walls with dense cytoplasm?
Scientific Resolution:
Meristematic cells are continuously and rapidly dividing embryonic cells. Vacuoles store food and maintain turgor in mature cells, which is unnecessary for actively dividing cells. Dense cytoplasm and thin walls facilitate rapid mitotic division without structural hindrance.
Histology 2: Sclerenchyma Toughness
Why are coconut husks and walnut shells extremely hard and rigid?
Scientific Resolution:
Coconut husks and walnut shells are composed of dead sclerenchyma tissue whose cell walls are heavily impregnated with lignin, an exceptionally rigid natural cement that provides immense mechanical strength and water impermeability.
