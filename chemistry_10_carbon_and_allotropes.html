Chemistry • Topic 10

Carbon & Its Allotropes

Allotropy definition, crystalline allotropes of carbon (Diamond: tetrahedral 3D network, Graphite: planar hexagonal layers, Fullerenes: geodesic spherical C60 cages), amorphous forms (coal, charcoal, coke, carbon black), and structural logic behind practical applications.

1. The Phenomenon of Allotropy

Imagine you have a large bucket of identical plastic building blocks. Using just those exact same blocks, you could build a tall, sturdy tower, or you could spread them out to build a wide, flat floor. The individual blocks are identical, but because they are connected differently, the final structures look completely different and serve entirely different purposes. In chemistry, carbon atoms act just like these blocks. When pure carbon atoms bond together in different ways, they create materials that look and behave very differently from one another.

Allotropy: The existence of an element in two or more different physical forms in the same physical state, which have different physical properties but identical chemical properties. The different forms are called allotropes.

• Logical Deduction: Because all allotropes of an element contain the exact same type of atoms, they show identical chemical behavior. For instance, burning equal masses of pure diamond, graphite, fullerene, or charcoal in excess oxygen will yield the exact same product—carbon dioxide gas (\(\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2\))—and leaving no residue behind.

2. Crystalline Allotropes: Diamond vs. Graphite

Carbon atoms can bond in highly organized, repeating geometric patterns to form crystalline solids. The sparkling diamond in a ring is the hardest naturally occurring substance on Earth, while graphite—the dark "lead" in your pencil—is so soft and slippery that it peels off onto paper as you write. This extreme difference is entirely due to their internal atomic architecture.

Tetrahedral vs. Planar Network: In Diamond, each carbon atom is tightly bound by strong covalent bonds to four other carbon atoms, forming a rigid, three-dimensional tetrahedral network. In Graphite, each carbon atom is covalently bonded to only three other carbon atoms, creating flat, planar hexagonal layers. Weak forces hold these layers together, allowing them to slide easily.
Property Diamond Graphite
Appearance Transparent, colorless, and sparkles brilliantly. Opaque, grayish-black, and slightly shiny.
Hardness Extremely hard (the hardest natural substance). Soft, slippery, and greasy to the touch.
Structure / Bonding Each carbon is bonded to 4 others (rigid 3D network). Each carbon is bonded to 3 others (flat hexagonal layers).
Electrical Conductivity Non-conductor (no free electrons available). Good conductor (the 4th valence electron is free to move).
Density High density (\(\approx 3.5\text{ g/cm}^3\)). Lower density (\(\approx 2.2\text{ g/cm}^3\)).

3. Fullerenes: The Spherical Molecular Allotropes

For centuries, scientists believed diamond and graphite were the only crystalline forms of carbon. In 1985, researchers discovered a third, entirely new class of crystalline carbon allotropes called fullerenes. The most famous and stable member of this family is Buckminsterfullerene (\(\text{C}_{60}\)), affectionately nicknamed "Buckyballs".

Buckminsterfullerene (\(\text{C}_{60}\)): A discrete molecular allotrope of carbon consisting of 60 carbon atoms arranged in a hollow, spherical cage resembling a geodesic soccer ball. It is named after the American architect R. Buckminster Fuller, who designed famous geodesic dome structures sharing this exact geometric symmetry.
Structural Features & Applications of \(\text{C}_{60}\)
1. Geometric Cage: The spherical cage is made of 60 carbon atoms interconnected into 20 hexagonal rings and 12 pentagonal rings, exactly like the stitching on a traditional football.

2. Discrete Molecules: Unlike diamond and graphite (which are gigantic continuous networks of infinite atoms), fullerenes are distinct, individual molecules that can dissolve in organic solvents like benzene.

3. High Tensile Strength & Conductivity: Because of the interlocking covalent rings, fullerenes can withstand extreme pressures and temperatures. They can behave as conductors or semiconductors.

4. Modern Nanotechnology Applications: The hollow interior can trap drug molecules for targeted cancer drug delivery inside the human body, serve as ultra-slick molecular ball-bearings/lubricants, and form the basis of carbon nanotubes.

4. Amorphous Forms of Carbon

Unlike the highly ordered crystalline forms, amorphous allotropes of carbon do not possess a regular, repeating atomic pattern. These are the dull, black powders and porous chunks you might see when wood or oil is burned. Think of the dark soot that coats the inside of a chimney, or the lightweight lumps of charcoal you use to light a barbecue grill. These materials are technically microcrystalline, but for our purposes, they lack large-scale geometric order.

Key Amorphous Allotropes & Their Origins
1. Coal: A natural fossil fuel formed over millions of years from decaying plant matter subjected to high heat and pressure underground.

2. Wood Charcoal: Produced by heating wood strongly in a limited supply of air (destructive distillation). It is highly porous and an excellent absorbent.

3. Coke: The solid residue left behind when coal is heated strongly in the absence of air. Used primarily as a reducing agent in metal extraction (like making iron).

4. Carbon Black (Soot): Produced by burning hydrocarbons (like oil or natural gas) in an insufficient supply of air. It is a fine black powder used as a pigment in black ink and automobile tires.

4. Worked Problems

Problem 4.1: Structure-Property Reasoning [3 Marks]
Graphite conducts electricity but Diamond does not, even though both are made entirely of carbon atoms. Explain this difference in terms of their atomic structure and bonding.
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. Carbon Valency: Carbon has 4 valence electrons available for bonding. [0.5 Marks]

2. Diamond Structure: In diamond, each carbon atom is covalently bonded to 4 other carbon atoms in a rigid 3D tetrahedral network. All valence electrons are locked into covalent bonds, leaving no free electrons to carry an electric charge. Hence, it is an insulator. [1.5 Marks]

3. Graphite Structure: In graphite, each carbon atom is bonded to only 3 other carbon atoms in a flat hexagonal layer. The 4th valence electron of each atom remains unbonded (delocalized) and is free to move throughout the layer. The flow of these free electrons allows graphite to conduct electricity. [1.0 Marks]
Problem 4.2: Uses Based on Structure [5 Marks]
Match each use to the correct allotrope (Diamond or Graphite):
(i) Electrodes in electrolysis
(ii) Cutting glass
(iii) Lubricant in heavy machinery
(iv) Gemstones in jewelry
(v) Pencil lead
Justify each answer with a structural reason.
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. (i) Electrodes: Graphite. Reason: It has free, delocalized electrons that make it a good conductor of electricity. [1 Mark]

2. (ii) Cutting glass: Diamond. Reason: The rigid 3D tetrahedral network of strong covalent bonds makes it incredibly hard. [1 Mark]

3. (iii) Lubricant: Graphite. Reason: The flat hexagonal layers are held together by weak forces, allowing them to slide over each other easily (slippery). [1 Mark]

4. (iv) Gemstones: Diamond. Reason: Its tightly packed, dense structure gives it a high refractive index, making it sparkle brilliantly when cut. [1 Mark]

5. (v) Pencil lead: Graphite. Reason: Because the layers slide and flake off easily due to weak forces, it leaves a dark mark on paper. [1 Mark]

5. Give Scientific Reasons

Conceptual Application
Give a scientific reason: Wood charcoal is often placed inside gas masks or air purifiers.
Examiner Solution:
Wood charcoal has a highly porous, amorphous structure. This gives it a massive internal surface area, allowing it to act as an excellent adsorbent. It easily attracts and traps harmful poison gases, odours, and impurities onto its surface, thereby purifying the air that passes through it. [2 Marks]
Conceptual Application
Give a scientific reason: Burning both diamond and coal produces carbon dioxide gas, but burning coal leaves behind ash while diamond does not.
Examiner Solution:
Diamond is an extremely pure crystalline allotrope of carbon; upon combustion, it completely reacts with oxygen to form only carbon dioxide gas, leaving zero solid residue. Coal, however, is a naturally occurring amorphous form that contains various mineral impurities (such as silica and clay) alongside carbon. When the carbon burns away as gas, these non-combustible impurities are left behind as ash. [2 Marks]
