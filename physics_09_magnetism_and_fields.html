Physics • Topic 09

Magnetism: Properties, Fields & Electromagnets

Properties of magnets (magnetic dipole, poles, repulsion as the only sure test), magnetic field lines (direction, non-intersection), methods of magnetisation, and the electromagnet.

1. Basic Properties and the Magnetic Dipole

Have you ever noticed how a fridge magnet snaps onto the metal door, but if you try to push two fridge magnets together, they sometimes fight back and twist away? This push-and-pull behavior happens because all magnets possess regions of concentrated strength known as poles, which always exist in opposing pairs. A magnet doesn't just pull things toward it; it interacts specifically with other magnetic objects based on how these poles are aligned. The Earth itself is a giant magnet, which is why a compass works! When you hold a bar magnet, you'll notice that the magnetic pull isn't uniform across its entire length; it is strongest at the very ends and weakest in the exact middle, also known as the neutral region.

Magnetic Poles & Dipole Property: The regions of a magnet where the magnetic strength is maximum are called its poles (North and South). The dipole property states that magnetic poles always exist in pairs; it is impossible to isolate a single North or South pole (a monopole). If a bar magnet is broken in half, each half instantly becomes a complete magnet with its own North and South poles.

• Directive Property: A freely suspended bar magnet always aligns itself in the geographic North-South direction. This alignment occurs because the Earth behaves like a giant bar magnet.
Property of Interaction Condition Resulting Force
Like Poles North faces North, OR South faces South. Repulsion (They strongly push each other away).
Unlike Poles North faces South. Attraction (They strongly pull toward each other).
Magnetic Materials Either pole facing unmagnetised iron/steel/cobalt/nickel. Attraction (By inducing opposite polarity in the material).
Non-Magnetic Materials Facing materials like wood, plastic, copper, gold, or brass. No Force (Zero interaction, the magnetic field passes through).

2. Repulsion: The Only Sure Test of Magnetism

A regular steel paperclip will easily jump to a magnet when brought near it. However, the paperclip itself is not a permanent magnet. When an unidentified metal bar attracts another magnet, it might be a magnet with opposite poles facing, or it might just be ordinary iron reacting to the magnetic pull. It takes another active magnet to actively push a magnet away. This means you can only confirm a piece of metal is a permanent magnet if it demonstrably repels another known magnet.

Magnetic Induction & Repulsion Test: Magnetic induction is the phenomenon where a magnetic material (like iron) acquires temporary magnetism when placed near a permanent magnet. The near end of the iron develops the opposite polarity to the inducing pole, leading to attraction. Because of induction, a magnet can attract an unmagnetised piece of iron. Therefore, attraction can occur between two magnets OR between a magnet and plain iron. However, repulsion can only ever occur between two like poles of permanent magnets. This makes repulsion the definitive diagnostic test for permanent magnetism.

3. Magnetic Field Lines

If you sprinkle iron filings around a bar magnet on a piece of paper and gently tap the paper, the filings will magically arrange themselves into beautiful, sweeping curves radiating outwards from the poles. The magnet exerts its invisible force in the three-dimensional space surrounding it. To help us scientifically analyse and visualise this unseen force, we draw lines that represent the paths the force follows. These mathematical curves show both the direction and the strength of the magnetic pull across space.

Magnetic Field & Field Lines: The entire space around a magnet within which its magnetic force of attraction or repulsion can be experienced by a magnetic compass needle or magnetic materials is called its magnetic field. The imaginary continuous curves used to represent this field graphically are called magnetic field lines.

• Key Property 1 (Directionality): Outside the magnet, magnetic field lines always travel continuously from the North pole to the South pole. Inside the magnet, they travel from South to North, forming closed loops.
• Key Property 2 (Non-intersection): Two magnetic field lines can never, ever intersect each other. If they did, a theoretical compass needle placed at the intersection point would have to point in two different directions at once, which is physically impossible.
• Key Property 3 (Crowding): The lines are crowded near the poles where the magnetic field is strongest, and spread far apart where the field is weak.

4. Methods of Magnetisation and Electromagnets

Scrapyard cranes use giant metallic discs to lift entire cars into the air, and then drop them effortlessly into a crusher at the flick of a switch. A normal permanent magnet cannot be switched off like this; once it holds a car, it wouldn't let go! By wrapping an electrical wire around a piece of iron, we can create a temporary magnet that is powered directly by electricity. This allows engineers to control extremely strong magnetic forces on demand.

The Electromagnet: An electromagnet is a powerful temporary magnet constructed by winding a tight coil of insulated copper wire around a soft iron core. When an electric current is passed through the coil, the iron core becomes heavily magnetised due to the magnetic effect of electric current. As soon as the current is switched off, the soft iron core loses almost all its magnetism instantaneously.

• Methods of Magnetisation: Aside from the electrical method (creating electromagnets), permanent magnets can be made by the single touch method (stroking a steel bar repeatedly with one pole of a magnet in the same direction) or the divided touch method (stroking from the centre outwards with opposite poles of two magnets).
Feature Permanent Magnet Electromagnet
Material Core Made of hard magnetic materials like steel or special alloys (Alnico, Neodymium). Made of temporary magnetic materials like soft iron.
Magnetism Duration Permanent (unless heavily heated, dropped, or hammered). Temporary (magnetism exists only while electric current flows).
Magnetic Strength Fixed strength that gradually weakens over a very long time. Relatively weak. Variable strength. Can be made extremely strong by altering the current.
Polarity Control Fixed polarity (North and South poles are permanent and cannot be swapped). Reversible polarity (poles can be reversed by changing the direction of the electric current).

5. Worked Problems

Problem 9.1: Magnetic Induction & Identification [3 Marks]
You are given two identical-looking metal rods, A and B. One is a permanent bar magnet and the other is an unmagnetised soft iron bar. Without using any other apparatus, explain step-by-step how you would identify which rod is the magnet.
Step-by-Step Solution:

1. Method of Magnetic Neutral Zone:
    A bar magnet has maximum magnetic attraction at its two poles (ends) and practically zero attraction at its centre (neutral region). An unmagnetised soft iron rod has uniform properties throughout.

2. Test Procedure:
    • Touch one end of rod A to the exact centre of rod B.
    • Case 1: If rod A is attracted to the centre of rod B, then rod A is the magnet (since the pole of rod A induces magnetism and attracts the iron at B's centre).
    • Case 2: If rod A is NOT attracted to the centre of rod B, then rod B is the magnet and rod A is the unmagnetised iron bar (since the centre of a magnet has no attraction power).
Problem 9.2: Electromagnet Coil Design [2 Marks]
An electromagnet is wound with 200 turns of insulated copper wire and connected to a 6 V battery, lifting 15 iron clips. What will happen to the lifting capacity if: (i) The number of turns is doubled to 400 turns with the same battery, and (ii) The battery voltage is increased to 12 V?
Step-by-Step Solution:

1. Case (i) Doubling Number of Turns (\(N\)):
    Magnetic field strength is directly proportional to the number of turns (\(B \propto N\)). Doubling turns increases magnetic field strength, significantly increasing the number of iron clips lifted.

2. Case (ii) Increasing Voltage to 12 V:
    By Ohm's Law (\(I = V/R\)), doubling voltage doubles the current (\(I\)). Since magnetic field strength is directly proportional to current (\(B \propto I\)), the electromagnet becomes much stronger and lifts more clips.

6. Give Scientific Reasons

Scientific Reasoning: Magnetism Test
Why is repulsion considered the only sure test of magnetism, whereas attraction is not? Give a clear scientific explanation based on magnetic properties.
Examiner Model Answer:

1. Why Attraction Fails: A permanent magnet has the ability to attract both an opposite pole of another magnet AND an ordinary, unmagnetised piece of magnetic material (like soft iron) through the process of magnetic induction. Thus, the phenomenon of attraction does not guarantee that the second object is actually a magnet. [1 Mark]

2. Why Repulsion Succeeds: Repulsion only ever occurs between two like poles (e.g., North facing North, or South facing South). Therefore, if an object actively repels a known permanent magnet, it must possess its own fixed magnetic poles, confirming definitively that it is indeed a permanent magnet. [1 Mark]
Application: Electromagnet Strength & Uses
State THREE distinct ways an engineer could increase the magnetic strength of a simple electromagnet. Give one practical real-world application of electromagnets.
Examiner Model Answer:

The strength of an electromagnet can be significantly increased by:
1. Increasing the magnitude of the electric current flowing through the copper coil (by using a stronger battery). [0.5 Marks]
2. Increasing the number of turns (windings) in the insulated copper coil wrapped around the core. [0.5 Marks]
3. Using a core made of soft iron instead of hard steel, as soft iron magnetises more easily, intensely, and rapidly. [1 Mark]

Practical Application: Electromagnets are heavily used in industrial scrap yard cranes to lift and transport heavy iron objects, in electric bells, in magnetic levitation (Maglev) trains, and in MRI machines in hospitals. [1 Mark]
Scientific Reasoning: Field Lines
Why do two magnetic field lines never intersect each other?
Examiner Model Answer:

1. The direction of a magnetic field at any given point is indicated by the tangent to the magnetic field line at that point, which is the direction a compass needle would point. [1 Mark]

2. If two magnetic field lines were to intersect, it would mean that at the point of intersection, the magnetic field has two different directions simultaneously. Since a single compass needle cannot point in two different directions at the exact same time, magnetic field lines can never intersect. [1 Mark]
Application: Making Permanent Magnets
A student tries to make a permanent magnet by wrapping a copper coil around a core and passing a direct current (DC) through it. What material should the core be made of, and why?
Examiner Model Answer:

The core should be made of steel (or an alloy like Alnico or Neodymium). [1 Mark]

Reason: While soft iron magnetises easily, it also demagnetises almost instantly when the current is turned off (making it good for electromagnets). Steel, being a hard magnetic material, retains a significant amount of its induced magnetism permanently even after the electric current is switched off. [1 Mark]
