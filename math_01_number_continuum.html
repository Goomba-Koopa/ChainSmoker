The Number Continuum: Integers, Fractions & Decimals

A journey through how our numbers grow: why humans had to invent negative numbers and fractions, the real-world common sense behind multiplying negative signs, how to squeeze infinite fractions into tiny gaps, and the secret prime test for terminating decimals.

Introduction: The Expanding Universe of Numbers

Imagine you are standing in an elevator in an infinitely tall skyscraper.

If you only ever travel up to the apartments, you only need positive counting numbers: Floor 1, Floor 2, Floor 3. For thousands of years, ancient civilizations got by with just these numbers because they were counting physical objects—baskets of wheat, sheep, or gold coins.

But what happens when you step out into the lobby? You are on Floor 0. And what happens when you press the elevator button to park your car in the underground levels? Suddenly, you are below the ground: Floor -1, Floor -2, Floor -3.

Now imagine you need to stop halfway between Floor 1 and Floor 2 because the elevator cables are being serviced at Floor \(1\frac{1}{2}\). You have just walked across the entire foundation of school mathematics: from natural counting numbers, to zero, to negative integers, and finally into fractions.

The Rational Number (In Everyday Terms) A rational number is any number that can be written as a simple fraction \(\frac{p}{q}\), where both the top number (\(p\)) and the bottom number (\(q\)) are whole integers, and the bottom number is not zero.

The word "rational" doesn't mean the number is sensible or logical—it comes from the word ratio. If you can write it as a ratio of two integers, it belongs in this family.

1. The Four Number Families: Boxes Inside Boxes

Instead of thinking of numbers as a boring list of definitions, think of them like a set of Russian nesting dolls. Each new family contains everything from the previous family, plus one powerful new ability:

Family Name What Members Live Here? The Superpower It Added The Problem It Couldn't Solve
Natural Numbers (\(\mathbb{N}\)) \(1, 2, 3, 4, 5, \dots\) Counting items you can touch and see. You cannot answer \(4 - 4\) (nothing left) or \(3 - 5\).
Whole Numbers (\(\mathbb{W}\)) \(0, 1, 2, 3, 4, \dots\) Introduced zero. Now we can represent an empty basket. Cannot show debt, freezing temperatures, or depths below sea level.
Integers (\(\mathbb{Z}\)) \(\dots, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\) Introduced negative numbers. Perfect for balances, scores, and opposite directions. Cannot divide things into parts. \(5 \div 2\) has no integer answer.
Rational Numbers (\(\mathbb{Q}\)) All fractions: \(\frac{1}{2}, -\frac{7}{4}, 0.75, -3\) Allows sharing, precise measurements, and division by any number except 0. Cannot represent numbers whose decimals never end and never repeat (like \(\pi = 3.14159\dots\)).
Wait, Is a Normal Number Like -4 Really a Fraction?

Yes! Any regular whole number or negative integer is secretly a fraction in disguise. You simply write it over \(1\):

$$7 = \frac{7}{1} \qquad -4 = \frac{-4}{1} \qquad 0 = \frac{0}{1}$$

That is why all integers belong to the Rational family. Every integer has a hidden denominator of \(1\).

⚠️ Common Student Trap: The Negative in the Denominator In the ICSE syllabus, a rational number is not in standard form if the negative sign is sitting on the bottom, like \(\frac{5}{-7}\). You must always shift the negative sign to the top or the front by multiplying top and bottom by \(-1\): $$\frac{5}{-7} = \frac{5 \times (-1)}{-7 \times (-1)} = \mathbf{-\frac{5}{7}}$$ Never leave a negative sign in the denominator on an exam paper!

2. Why Does Negative × Negative = Positive?

Almost every student has been told: "Minus times minus gives plus. Don't ask questions, just remember it." But that feels completely counterintuitive. Why should multiplying two negatives suddenly turn into a positive?

There are two ways to understand this so it makes complete, unbreakable sense:

Way 1: The Number Pattern (The Staircase)

Mathematics must follow patterns without breaking. Watch what happens when we multiply \(-4\) by a series of numbers that decrease by 1 at every step:

The Downward Staircase Pattern
$$-4 \times 3 = -12$$ $$-4 \times 2 = -8 \quad \text{(notice: this is } 4 \text{ greater than } -12\text{)}$$ $$-4 \times 1 = -4 \quad \text{(again, adds } 4\text{)}$$ $$-4 \times 0 = \phantom{-}0 \quad \text{(again, adds } 4\text{)}$$
Now ask yourself: if the pattern continues to add 4 each time we decrease the multiplier by 1, what must come next? $$-4 \times (-1) = \mathbf{+4}$$ $$-4 \times (-2) = \mathbf{+8}$$ $$-4 \times (-3) = \mathbf{+12}$$ If \(-4 \times -3\) gave \(-12\), the staircase pattern would suddenly snap and break! Mathematics demands that the pattern continues smoothly.

Way 2: Real Life (The Bank Balance & The Movie Tape)

Think of your bank balance. If someone adds money to your account, that's positive. If someone takes money away, that's negative.

  • Positive × Negative: You get 3 parking fines of ₹200 each. You added 3 debts: \(3 \times (-200) = \mathbf{-600}\) (You lost ₹600).
  • Negative × Negative: Now suppose the city realizes the parking tickets were issued by mistake, and they cancel (remove) all 3 fines. They are taking away (negative) your debt (negative). Your bank balance goes up by ₹600!

$$\text{Removing a debt} = -(-600) = \mathbf{+600}$$

3. Order of Operations: Taming Nested Brackets

When an arithmetic problem has multiple signs, brackets, and fractions, students often rush from left to right and get the wrong answer. Mathematics has a strict traffic code called BODMAS:

The BODMAS Priority Ladder
  1. B — Brackets: Always clear inside the brackets first.
  2. O — Of (or Orders/Powers): "Of" means multiplication (\(\frac{1}{2}\) of \(20 = 10\)), handled before regular division.
  3. D & M — Division & Multiplication: Equal rank, solved left-to-right.
  4. A & S — Addition & Subtraction: Equal rank, solved left-to-right.

The Four Layers of Brackets (Inside-Out Rule)

In Class 7 ICSE, you will see problems with up to four nested layers of brackets. Always work like peeling an onion, from the innermost layer outwards:

  1. 1st: The Vinculum or Bar (\(\overline{a - b}\)): The horizontal line over numbers. It acts like the tightest bracket!
  2. 2nd: Parentheses or Round Brackets: \(( \quad )\)
  3. 3rd: Braces or Curly Brackets: \(\{ \quad \}\)
  4. 4th: Square Brackets: \([ \quad ]\)
⚠️ The Negative Sign Outside Brackets When you see a minus sign directly in front of a bracket, like \(-\left(x - 4\right)\), remember that the minus applies to every term inside when the bracket is removed: $$-\left(x - 4\right) = -x + 4$$ A shocking number of exam marks are lost because students write \(-x - 4\) instead!

4. Squeezing Fractions: The "Sand vs. Stepping Stones" Idea

If you look at the whole numbers on a ruler: \(1, 2, 3, 4\dots\) they are like stepping stones across a pond. Between stone 2 and stone 3, there is open water. There are no whole numbers between 2 and 3.

Rational numbers (fractions) are completely different. They are like fine sand. No matter how closely you pack two grains of sand together, if you look under a microscope, there is always room to drop another grain of sand between them.

The Density Property Between any two distinct rational numbers, there exist infinitely many other rational numbers. You can never have two rational numbers that are "next to each other."

How to Find Fractions in the Middle (The Stretched Denominator Method)

Suppose an exam asks: "Find 5 rational numbers between \(\frac{1}{4}\) and \(\frac{2}{3}\)."

Here is the foolproof 3-step method:

  1. Step 1: Get a common denominator. The LCM of 4 and 3 is 12:
    $$\frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{12} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{2}{3} = \frac{8}{12}$$
  2. Step 2: Check the room between numerators. Between \(3\) and \(8\), we have \(4, 5, 6, 7\). That's 4 numbers, but the question asked for 5!
  3. Step 3: Stretch the fractions! Multiply top and bottom of both fractions by 10 (or 2):
    $$\frac{3 \times 10}{12 \times 10} = \frac{30}{120} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{8 \times 10}{12 \times 10} = \frac{80}{120}$$ Now look at the huge gap: any numbers from \(\frac{31}{120}\) to \(\frac{79}{120}\) are perfectly valid answers!

5. Decimals: Will It Stop, or Loop Forever?

Every fraction can be turned into a decimal by dividing the top number by the bottom number. When you do the long division, only two things can ever happen:

  1. It terminates: The division ends with a remainder of 0 (e.g., \(\frac{3}{8} = 0.375\)).
  2. It repeats (recurs): The remainders start cycling in a loop, so the decimal digits repeat endlessly (e.g., \(\frac{1}{3} = 0.333\dots\) or \(\frac{5}{11} = 0.4545\dots\)).

The Secret Prime Test (No Division Needed!)

Did you know you can tell whether a fraction will terminate or repeat in 5 seconds without doing any division at all?

The 2 and 5 Prime Factor Rule First, reduce the fraction to its simplest lowest terms (cancel out common factors).
Then look at the prime factors of the denominator (bottom number):
  • If the denominator breaks down into only 2s, only 5s, or a mix of 2s and 5s, the decimal will always terminate (stop)!
  • If the denominator contains even one other prime number (like 3, 7, 11, or 13), the decimal will loop forever (repeat)!
Fraction Simplest Form Denominator Factors Verdict Decimal Value
\(\frac{7}{40}\) Already in simplest form \(40 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 5\) (only 2s and 5s) Terminating \(0.175\)
\(\frac{9}{24}\) Reduces to \(\frac{3}{8}\) \(8 = 2 \times 2 \times 2\) (only 2s) Terminating \(0.375\)
\(\frac{5}{12}\) Already in simplest form \(12 = 2 \times 2 \times 3\) (contains a 3!) Repeating \(0.41\overline{6}\)
\(\frac{2}{7}\) Already in simplest form \(7 = 7\) (contains a 7!) Repeating \(0.\overline{285714}\dots\)
Why 2 and 5? Why Not 3 or 4?

Because humans have 10 fingers, our world runs on a Base-10 decimal system. Every decimal column represents a power of 10: tenths (\(\frac{1}{10}\)), hundredths (\(\frac{1}{100}\)), thousandths (\(\frac{1}{1000}\)).

What are the only prime numbers that can multiply to make 10? Just 2 and 5! (\(2 \times 5 = 10\)). If a denominator has factors of 2 and 5, you can always multiply it into a neat power of 10. If it has a factor like 3 or 7, it can never evenly fit into 10, 100, or 1000, so it spills over forever into an infinite loop.

Worked Problems

Problem 1.1: Multi-Tier Nested BODMAS Reduction ICSE Exam Standard • 4 Marks
Evaluate the following expression step-by-step, peeling the brackets from the inside out: $$-15 - \left[ -8 + \left\{ 4 - \left( 3 - \overline{7 - 2} \right) \right\} \right]$$
The Plan: Clear the vinculum (bar) first, then round brackets, then curly braces, then square brackets.

Step 1: Evaluate under the bar (\(\overline{7 - 2}\)):
\(7 - 2 = 5\)
Now write down the whole line with that part replaced:
\(= -15 - \left[ -8 + \left\{ 4 - ( 3 - 5 ) \right\} \right]\)

Step 2: Evaluate the round brackets \((3 - 5)\):
\(3 - 5 = -2\)
Now the expression becomes:
\(= -15 - \left[ -8 + \left\{ 4 - (-2) \right\} \right]\)
Remember: subtracting a negative is the same as adding a positive (\(4 - (-2) = 4 + 2 = 6\)):
\(= -15 - \left[ -8 + 6 \right]\)

Step 3: Evaluate the square brackets \([-8 + 6]\):
\(-8 + 6 = -2\)
Now the expression is simplified down to:
\(= -15 - [-2]\)

Step 4: Final calculation:
\(-15 - (-2) = -15 + 2 = \mathbf{-13}\)
Problem 1.2: Inserting Fractions Between Negative Limits ICSE Exam Standard • 3 Marks
Insert exactly five rational numbers between \(-\frac{2}{3}\) and \(-\frac{1}{4}\). Arrange your final answers in ascending order.
Step 1: Make the denominators identical with LCM:
The LCM of 3 and 4 is 12.
$$-\frac{2}{3} = -\frac{2 \times 4}{3 \times 4} = -\frac{8}{12}$$ $$-\frac{1}{4} = -\frac{1 \times 3}{4 \times 3} = -\frac{3}{12}$$
Step 2: Check the available integer gap:
Between \(-8\) and \(-3\), we have \(-7, -6, -5, -4\). That is only 4 integers, but we need 5 numbers.

Step 3: Stretch the fractions wider:
Multiply both fractions top and bottom by 2:
$$-\frac{8 \times 2}{12 \times 2} = -\frac{16}{24}$$ $$-\frac{3 \times 2}{12 \times 2} = -\frac{6}{24}$$
Step 4: Select five numbers in ascending order (from most negative to least negative):
Between \(-16\) and \(-6\), we can easily pick:
$$-\frac{15}{24}, -\frac{14}{24}, -\frac{13}{24}, -\frac{12}{24}, -\frac{11}{24}$$
Step 5: Reduce fractions to simplest form:
$$\mathbf{-\frac{5}{8}, -\frac{7}{12}, -\frac{13}{24}, -\frac{1}{2}, -\frac{11}{24}}$$
Problem 1.3: Converting a Repeating Decimal to a Fraction ICSE Exam Standard • 4 Marks
Convert the repeating decimal \(0.3\overline{45}\) (which means \(0.3454545\dots\) where 45 repeats forever) into a regular fraction in lowest terms. Then check the bottom number to see why it repeated.
The Strategy: We want to multiply by powers of 10 to line up the repeating \(.454545\dots\) tails so they subtract and cancel out to zero!

Step 1: Give our repeating decimal the name \(x\):
Let \(x = 0.3454545\dots\)   — [Equation 1]

Step 2: Move the non-repeating digit (3) across the decimal point:
Multiply Equation 1 by 10:
$$10x = 3.454545\dots$$   — [Equation 2]

Step 3: Move one full repeating block (45) across the decimal point:
Since 2 digits repeat, multiply Equation 2 by 100 (which is \(10 \times 100 = 1000\)):
$$1000x = 345.454545\dots$$   — [Equation 3]

Step 4: Subtract Equation 2 from Equation 3:
Notice that both equations now have the exact same infinite tail \(.454545\dots\) after the decimal point. When we subtract, the tails vanish completely!
$$1000x - 10x = 345.4545\dots - 3.4545\dots$$ $$990x = 342$$ $$x = \frac{342}{990}$$
Step 5: Reduce to lowest terms:
Divide numerator and denominator by their common factor 18:
$$x = \frac{342 \div 18}{990 \div 18} = \mathbf{\frac{19}{55}}$$
Why did it repeat? Look at the denominator: \(55 = 5 \times 11\). Because it has an 11 in it (not just 2s or 5s), it could never fit into Base-10, so it was guaranteed to loop forever!

Mathematical Curiosities & "Why?" Questions

Curiosity 1.1: Why Can't We Divide by Zero?
Why does \(0 \div 5 = 0\), but \(5 \div 0\) causes your calculator to scream "ERROR"?
Think of division as reverse multiplication: \(A \div B = C\) means that \(B \times C\) must equal \(A\).

For \(0 \div 5\):
You are asking: "What number times 5 gives 0?"
Easy! \(5 \times 0 = 0\). The answer is clearly and cleanly 0.

For \(5 \div 0\):
You are asking: "What number times 0 gives 5?"
Think about it. \(0 \times 1 = 0\), \(0 \times 100 = 0\), \(0 \times 1,000,000 = 0\). Nothing in the universe multiplied by 0 can ever equal 5! There is literally no answer, so we say it is undefined.
Curiosity 1.2: Why Are 2 and 5 So Special in Decimals?
Why do only denominators with factors of 2 and 5 stop, while fractions with 3 or 7 loop endlessly?
Because humans have 10 fingers, our entire number system is Base-10.

Every decimal position is based on powers of 10: tenths (\(\frac{1}{10}\)), hundredths (\(\frac{1}{100}\)), thousandths (\(\frac{1}{1000}\)).

What are the only prime numbers that build 10? Just 2 and 5 (\(2 \times 5 = 10\)).
If a fraction's denominator has only 2s and 5s, you can always multiply it into a 10, 100, or 1000. For example: $$\frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2 \times 2} \xrightarrow{\times 25} \frac{25}{100} = 0.25$$ But no matter what whole number you multiply 3 or 7 by, you can never turn it into 10, 100, or 1000. It simply can't fit into our Base-10 system, so it spills over into an endless repeating loop!
