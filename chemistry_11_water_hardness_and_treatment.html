Chemistry • Topic 11

Water Hardness & Treatment Technologies

Soft vs Hard water, Chemical origin of hardness (\(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) and \(\text{Mg}^{2+}\) salts), Temporary vs Permanent hardness, Removal methods (Boiling, Clark's process, Washing soda, Ion exchange resins), and Industrial boiler scale hazards.

1. Soft Water vs. Hard Water

Have you ever noticed that when washing your hands with soap in certain cities or when using mountain well water, the soap easily foams into rich, fluffy bubbles (lather), while in other areas, the soap refuses to foam and instead forms a sticky, curd-like white scum that clings to your skin? This difference is caused by dissolved mineral salts in the water.

Definitions of Water Types: • Soft Water: Water that readily forms a rich lather when rubbed with ordinary soap (e.g. distilled water, pure rainwater). It contains negligible amounts of dissolved calcium and magnesium ions.
• Hard Water: Water that does not readily produce lather with soap, but instead reacts with soap molecules to form an insoluble white precipitate called scum (e.g. underground well water, tube-well water, water flowing through limestone hills).
The Chemical Mechanism of Soap Scum Formation
Ordinary bathing soap is the sodium salt of a long-chain fatty acid called sodium stearate (\(\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COONa}\)). When soap dissolves in hard water containing dissolved calcium ions (\(\text{Ca}^{2+}\)), a double displacement reaction takes place: $$2\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COONa (aq)} + \text{CaSO}_4\text{ (aq)} \rightarrow (\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO})_2\text{Ca}\downarrow\text{ (Curdy Scum)} + \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4\text{ (aq)}$$ Key Practical Consequence: No lather can form until all the dissolved calcium and magnesium ions have precipitated out as scum, resulting in massive wastage of soap.

2. Temporary Hardness vs. Permanent Hardness

Water hardness is categorized according to the specific chemical anions paired with the calcium and magnesium cations:

Parameter Temporary Hardness Permanent Hardness
Dissolved Chemical Salts Dissolved Hydrogen Carbonates (Bicarbonates) of Calcium and Magnesium:
• Calcium Bicarbonate \(\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2\)
• Magnesium Bicarbonate \(\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2\)		 Dissolved Chlorides and Sulphates of Calcium and Magnesium:
• Calcium Chloride \(\text{CaCl}_2\) & Calcium Sulphate \(\text{CaSO}_4\)
• Magnesium Chloride \(\text{MgCl}_2\) & Magnesium Sulphate \(\text{MgSO}_4\)
Origin in Nature Rainwater containing dissolved \(\text{CO}_2\) percolating through limestone (\(\text{CaCO}_3\)) rocks. Water flowing through underground mineral deposits of gypsum (\(\text{CaSO}_4\cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O}\)) or rock salt.
Ease of Removal Can be removed simply by boiling or adding calculated slaked lime (Clark's process). Cannot be removed by simple boiling; requires chemical treatment or ion-exchange resins.

3. Methods for Softening Hard Water

Softening water means removing or deactivating the dissolved \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) and \(\text{Mg}^{2+}\) ions so that the water can lather freely with soap:

Softening Chemical Reactions
1. Removal of Temporary Hardness by Boiling:
Heating decomposes soluble bicarbonates into insoluble carbonates that precipitate out as solid crusts:
$$\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2\text{ (aq)} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \mathbf{\text{CaCO}_3\downarrow\text{ (Solid Insoluble)}} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2\uparrow$$
2. Clark's Process (Addition of Slaked Lime):
A carefully calculated quantity of calcium hydroxide \(\text{Ca(OH)}_2\) is added to neutralize temporary hardness without heat:
$$\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2 + \text{Ca(OH)}_2 \rightarrow 2\mathbf{\text{CaCO}_3\downarrow} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
3. Removal of Permanent Hardness using Washing Soda (\(\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3\)):
Adding sodium carbonate converts soluble sulphates/chlorides into insoluble metal carbonates:
$$\text{CaSO}_4\text{ (aq)} + \text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3\text{ (aq)} \rightarrow \mathbf{\text{CaCO}_3\downarrow} + \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4\text{ (aq)}$$ $$\text{MgCl}_2\text{ (aq)} + \text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3\text{ (aq)} \rightarrow \mathbf{\text{MgCO}_3\downarrow} + 2\text{NaCl (aq)}$$
4. Modern Ion-Exchange (Permutit / Zeolite Process):
Hard water is passed through a cylinder packed with sodium aluminium silicate zeolite (\(\text{Na}_2\text{Z}\)). The resin swaps its harmless sodium ions for the calcium and magnesium ions in the water: $$\text{Na}_2\text{Z (Resin)} + \text{Ca}^{2+}\text{ (aq)} \rightarrow \mathbf{\text{CaZ (Trapped)}} + 2\text{Na}^+\text{ (Soft Water)}$$
[†1] Industrial Hazard: Boiler Scale & Explosion Risks When hard water is fed into high-pressure industrial steam boilers, the continuous boiling creates a thick, stony layer of \(\text{CaCO}_3\) and \(\text{CaSO}_4\) on the inner metal walls known as boiler scale. Because scale is an extremely poor conductor of heat, operators must burn excessive coal to boil water. Worse, if the brittle scale cracks under heat, cold water suddenly contacts the red-hot steel boiler plate, creating instantaneous high-pressure steam that can explode the entire boiler!

4. Worked Problems

Problem 11.1: Chemical Identification of Water Samples
A chemistry student is given three unlabeled beakers containing Sample \(A\), Sample \(B\), and Sample \(C\).
• Sample \(A\) lathers immediately with soap without any treatment.
• Sample \(B\) forms scum initially, but after boiling for 5 minutes, it filters clear and lathers freely.
• Sample \(C\) forms scum initially, and boiling has zero effect on its ability to lather.
Classify the three samples and identify the chemical salts responsible for hardness in \(B\) and \(C\).
Step-by-Step Logical Diagnosis:

1. Sample \(A\) is Soft Water: Because it produces rich lather immediately without treatment, it contains negligible dissolved \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) or \(\text{Mg}^{2+}\) ions.

2. Sample \(B\) is Temporarily Hard Water: Because boiling successfully removes its hardness, the hardness is caused by thermally unstable Calcium Bicarbonate \(\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2\) or Magnesium Bicarbonate \(\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2\), which decompose upon heating to form insoluble carbonate precipitates.

3. Sample \(C\) is Permanently Hard Water: Because boiling fails to soften the water, the hardness is caused by thermally stable Chlorides or Sulphates of Calcium/Magnesium (\(\text{CaCl}_2, \text{CaSO}_4, \text{MgCl}_2, \text{MgSO}_4\)).
Problem 11.2: The Kettle Crust Phenomenon
In households supplied with well water, the inner heating element of an electric kettle develops a hard white chalky coating after a few weeks of use. Explain: (i) What this white substance is chemically, (ii) The balanced chemical reaction explaining its formation, and (iii) How dilute lemon juice or vinegar safely dissolves it.
Step-by-Step Chemical Resolution:

1. Identity: The white coating is solid Calcium Carbonate (\(\text{CaCO}_3\)) (scale/fur).

2. Formation Equation: As tap water is boiled in the kettle, soluble calcium bicarbonate decomposes thermally: $$\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2\text{ (aq)} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \mathbf{\text{CaCO}_3\downarrow\text{ (Solid Fur)}} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2\uparrow$$
3. Descaling with Acid: Lemon juice (citric acid) or vinegar (acetic acid) contains mild acids (\(\text{H}^+\)) that react with the insoluble carbonate, converting it into soluble calcium citrate/acetate along with harmless fizzing of \(\text{CO}_2\): $$\text{CaCO}_3\text{ (s)} + 2\text{CH}_3\text{COOH (aq)} \rightarrow (\text{CH}_3\text{COO})_2\text{Ca (aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2\uparrow$$

5. Give Scientific Reasons

Water Hardness 1: Boiling Temporary Hard Water
Why can temporary hardness of water be removed by simple boiling, while permanent hardness cannot?
Scientific Resolution:
Temporary hardness is caused by soluble bicarbonates (\(\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2\) and \(\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2\)), which decompose upon heating into insoluble carbonates (\(\text{CaCO}_3\)) that precipitate out. Permanent hardness is caused by soluble chlorides and sulphates (\(\text{CaCl}_2, \text{MgSO}_4\)), which are thermally stable and do not decompose upon boiling.
Water Hardness 2: Detergent vs Soap in Hard Water
Why are synthetic detergents preferred over ordinary soaps for washing fabrics in hard water?
Scientific Resolution:
Soap reacts with calcium and magnesium ions in hard water to form a sticky, insoluble precipitate called scum (\(\text{curd}\)), wasting soap. Synthetic detergents do not form insoluble precipitates with \(\text{Ca}^{2+}\) or \(\text{Mg}^{2+}\) ions and lather freely even in hard water.
