Work, Energy & Simple Machines

The scalar definition of work (W = F × s), kinetic and potential energy, conservation of energy, and the three classes of levers with mechanical advantage.

1. The Scientific Definition of Work

If you push against a solid brick wall for an hour, you will undoubtedly feel exhausted. However, in the strict language of physics, you have done no work. Work is only accomplished when a force successfully causes an object to move. If you push a shopping cart down an aisle, the force you apply causes displacement, meaning work is being done. The amount of work depends entirely on how much force is applied and how far the object travels in the direction of that force.

Work (\(W\)): Work is said to be done when a force applied on a body produces motion in the body. It is formally defined as the product of the magnitude of the force applied and the displacement of the body in the direction of the force.

• Crucial Examiner Insight: Work is a scalar quantity. Even though it is the product of two vector quantities (force and displacement), it only has a magnitude and no specific direction. If displacement is zero (e.g., pushing a fixed wall), the work done is strictly zero regardless of the force applied.
The Formula for Work
The mathematical expression for work is given by:
    $$W = F \times s$$
Where:
    • \(W\) = Work done (measured in Joules, J)
    • \(F\) = Force applied (measured in Newtons, N)
    • \(s\) = Displacement in the direction of the force (measured in metres, m)

Note: 1 Joule of work is done when a force of 1 Newton moves a body through a distance of 1 metre in its own direction.

2. Kinetic and Potential Energy

Energy is the capacity to do work. Think of a rubber band: when it rests on a table, it has no active energy. But if you stretch it back like a catapult, it now stores energy because of its new shape—it has the potential to snap forward. Once released, the band flies through the air; its stored energy transforms into the energy of motion. These two states represent the two primary forms of mechanical energy in our universe.

Potential Energy (PE): The energy possessed by a body by virtue of its specific position or deformed state. (e.g., water stored in a high dam, a compressed spring). Formula: \(PE = m \times g \times h\).

Kinetic Energy (KE): The energy possessed by a body by virtue of its state of motion. (e.g., a speeding train, a rolling stone). Formula: \(KE = \frac{1}{2} m v^2\).
Characteristic Potential Energy (PE) Kinetic Energy (KE)
Origin Depends entirely on the object's position, height, or physical configuration (shape). Depends entirely on the object's velocity and motion.
Formula \(PE = mgh\) (Gravitational PE) \(KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\)
State at Rest Can be maximum when the body is at rest at its highest position. Always zero when the body is at rest (\(v = 0\)).
Example A wound-up clock spring; a boulder balanced on the edge of a cliff. A fast-moving cricket ball; wind blowing through turbines.

3. The Law of Conservation of Energy

Imagine a roller coaster perched at the very top of a steep drop. At this moment, it has maximum potential energy and almost no kinetic energy. As it plunges downwards, it loses height (losing potential energy) but gains immense speed (gaining kinetic energy). The energy isn't vanishing; it is merely changing its disguise. This absolute rule governs every physical process in the universe.

Law of Conservation of Energy: Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transformed from one form to another. The total energy of an isolated system always remains constant.

• Crucial Examiner Insight: In ideal mechanical systems with no friction, the sum of Potential Energy and Kinetic Energy (Total Mechanical Energy) is perfectly constant at all points during motion.

4. Simple Machines and Levers

We use machines every day to make tasks easier. When you use a spoon to pry open a tight lid, or a wheelbarrow to carry heavy soil, you are using a simple machine. A machine does not create energy, but it allows us to apply a smaller force over a longer distance to overcome a larger resistive force. One of the most fundamental simple machines is the lever—a rigid bar that pivots around a fixed point.

Lever: A rigid, straight, or bent bar which is capable of turning about a fixed axis or point called the fulcrum (F). The force applied to the lever is the Effort (E), and the resistive force to be overcome is the Load (L).
Class of Lever Configuration Structure Mechanical Advantage (MA) Real-World Examples
Class I Fulcrum (F) is located exactly between the Load (L) and the Effort (E). Can be \(> 1\), \(= 1\), or \(< 1\) depending on the lengths of the arms. Seesaw, crowbar, scissors, pair of pliers.
Class II Load (L) is located exactly between the Fulcrum (F) and the Effort (E). Always \(> 1\) (acts as a force multiplier). Wheelbarrow, nutcracker, bottle opener.
Class III Effort (E) is located exactly between the Fulcrum (F) and the Load (L). Always \(< 1\) (acts as a speed multiplier). Pair of tongs, tweezers, human forearm lifting a weight.

5. Mechanical Advantage

How do we mathematically measure if a machine is actually helping us? If a lever allows you to lift a massive 1000 Newton boulder by only pushing down with 200 Newtons of effort, the lever is multiplying your force by five. This ratio is the machine's mechanical advantage, which dictates its real-world utility.

Mechanical Advantage (MA): The ratio of the load (the resistive force overcome by the machine) to the effort (the force applied to the machine).
Mechanical Advantage Formula
    $$MA = \frac{\text{Load (L)}}{\text{Effort (E)}}$$
Because MA is a ratio of two forces (both measured in Newtons), it has no unit.

Interpretation:
• If \(MA > 1\): The machine acts as a force multiplier. (e.g., a Class II lever like a nutcracker).
• If \(MA < 1\): The machine acts as a speed multiplier. (e.g., a Class III lever like a broom).
• If \(MA = 1\): The machine only changes the direction of effort. (e.g., a single fixed pulley).

6. Give Scientific Reasons

Q1: Why is the mechanical advantage of a Class II lever always greater than 1?
In a Class II lever, the load is situated between the fulcrum and the effort. Because of this structural arrangement, the effort arm (distance from fulcrum to effort) is always strictly longer than the load arm (distance from fulcrum to load). Since by the principle of moments \(MA = \frac{\text{Effort Arm}}{\text{Load Arm}}\), dividing a larger number by a smaller number mathematically guarantees an MA strictly greater than 1.
Q2: A porter standing still with a heavy suitcase on his head does absolutely no work in the eyes of physics. Why?
Work is defined scientifically as the product of force and displacement (\(W = F \times s\)). Even though the porter exerts an upward muscular force to support the heavy suitcase against gravity, he is standing completely still. Therefore, the displacement (\(s\)) is zero. Substituting this into the formula yields \(W = F \times 0 = 0\text{ Joules}\). Thus, zero physical work is accomplished.

7. Worked Problems

Problem 2.1: Work-Energy Calculation [3 Marks]
A crane lifts a block of mass \(50\text{ kg}\) vertically upwards through a height of \(8\text{ m}\). Calculate: (i) the work done by the crane, and (ii) the potential energy gained by the block. (Take \(g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\))
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. Identify given values:
    Mass (\(m\)) = \(50\text{ kg}\)
    Displacement / Height (\(s\) or \(h\)) = \(8\text{ m}\)
    Acceleration due to gravity (\(g\)) = \(10\text{ m/s}^2\)

2. Part (i): Work Done (\(W\)):
    Force applied by crane to lift block = Weight of block = \(m \times g\)
    \(F = 50 \times 10 = 500\text{ N}\)
    \(W = F \times s = 500\text{ N} \times 8\text{ m} = \mathbf{4000\text{ J}}\) [1.5 Marks]

3. Part (ii): Potential Energy (PE):
    \(PE = m \times g \times h\)
    \(PE = 50 \times 10 \times 8 = \mathbf{4000\text{ J}}\) [1.5 Marks]

(Examiner Note: This beautifully illustrates the Work-Energy Theorem—the work done on the object is exactly equal to the energy stored within it.)
Problem 2.2: Lever Mechanical Advantage [3 Marks]
A Class II lever has a mechanical advantage of 3. What effort is required to lift a heavy stone load of \(600\text{ N}\)? If the effort arm is \(1.5\text{ m}\) long, calculate the length of the load arm.
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. Part (i): Calculating required Effort:
    Given: \(MA = 3\), \(\text{Load (L)} = 600\text{ N}\)
    Formula: \(MA = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{Effort}}\)
    \(3 = \frac{600}{E}\)
    \(E = \frac{600}{3} = \mathbf{200\text{ N}}\) [1.5 Marks]

2. Part (ii): Calculating Load Arm length:
    Given: \(\text{Effort Arm} = 1.5\text{ m}\)
    By Principle of Moments: \(MA = \frac{\text{Effort Arm}}{\text{Load Arm}}\)
    \(3 = \frac{1.5}{\text{Load Arm}}\)
    \(\text{Load Arm} = \frac{1.5}{3} = \mathbf{0.5\text{ m}}\) [1.5 Marks]
