Force, Pressure & Pascal's Principle

Thrust vs pressure (P = F/A), hydrostatic pressure derivation (P = hρg), Pascal's Principle and the hydraulic press, atmospheric pressure and the barometer.

1. Thrust and Pressure

Have you ever noticed how a sharp knife easily slices through a firm vegetable, while a blunt knife requires you to push much harder without making a dent? Even if you apply the exact same amount of force with your hand, the outcome is completely different. This happens because the sharp knife concentrates all your effort into a very tiny area, creating a much more intense effect. This effect of force spread over an area is what we call pressure.

Thrust and Pressure: Thrust is the total force acting perpendicular to a surface. It is a vector quantity, measured in Newtons (\(N\)).

Pressure is the thrust acting per unit area of a surface. It tells us how concentrated the force is. The SI unit of pressure is the Pascal (\(Pa\)), where \(1\text{ Pa} = 1\text{ N/m}^2\).
Mathematical Formula for Pressure
The fundamental relationship between pressure (\(P\)), thrust or force (\(F\)), and the contact area (\(A\)) is given by: $$ P = \frac{F}{A} $$ • Direct relationship: Pressure increases if the applied force increases.
• Inverse relationship: Pressure increases drastically when the contact area decreases (which is why needles and pins have very sharp, tiny points).
Characteristic Thrust (\(F\)) Pressure (\(P\))
Meaning Total perpendicular force acting on a whole surface. Force acting per unit area of the surface.
Dependency on Area Independent of the area over which it acts. Inversely proportional to the area of contact.
Quantity Type Vector quantity (has magnitude and direction). Scalar quantity (acts uniformly in all directions at a point in fluids).
SI Unit Newton (\(N\)) Pascal (\(Pa\)) or Newton per square metre (\(N/m^2\))

2. Hydrostatic Pressure in Liquids

If you have ever dived deep to the bottom of a swimming pool, you might have felt a squeezing sensation or pain in your ears. This happens because water is heavy. The deeper you go, the more water there is stacked up directly above you, pushing down due to gravity. Liquids exert pressure on the bottom and walls of their container, and this pressure grows significantly as depth increases.

Hydrostatic Pressure: The pressure exerted by a static fluid (liquid or gas) at rest due to the weight of the fluid column above it. Inside a liquid, pressure acts equally in all directions and does not depend on the shape of the container.
Derivation of Liquid Pressure (\(P = h\rho g\))
Consider a liquid of uniform density \(\rho\) in a container. Imagine a vertical column of this liquid with a cross-sectional area \(A\) and a depth \(h\).

1. Volume of the liquid column (\(V\)): \(V = \text{Area} \times \text{height} = A \times h\)
2. Mass of the liquid (\(m\)): \(\text{Mass} = \text{Volume} \times \text{Density} \Rightarrow m = (A \times h) \times \rho\)
3. Weight or Thrust exerted at the bottom (\(F\)): \(F = m \times g = A \times h \times \rho \times g\)
4. Pressure (\(P\)): $$ P = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{A \cdot h \cdot \rho \cdot g}{A} = h\rho g $$
Conclusion: Liquid pressure at a point is directly proportional to depth (\(h\)), liquid density (\(\rho\)), and acceleration due to gravity (\(g\)).

3. Pascal's Principle and The Hydraulic Press

In a modern garage, a mechanic can lift a heavy, multi-ton car simply by pressing down on a small lever or button. This seems like magic, but it uses enclosed fluids to multiply force. If you squeeze a closed tube of toothpaste at the bottom, the pressure transmits perfectly through the paste, forcing it out the top. Fluids in a closed system transmit pressure everywhere without losing any intensity.

Pascal's Principle: When pressure is applied to an enclosed static fluid, it is transmitted undiminished (equally) to every part of the fluid and to the walls of the containing vessel.
The Hydraulic Press (Force Multiplier)
A hydraulic press consists of two cylinders of different cross-sectional areas connected by a tube and filled with an incompressible liquid.

Let the small piston have area \(A_1\) and the large piston have area \(A_2\).
A small effort force \(F_1\) is applied to the small piston.

1. Pressure created: \(P = \frac{F_1}{A_1}\)
2. By Pascal's Principle, this exact pressure \(P\) reaches the large piston.
3. Upward Force on large piston (\(F_2\)): \(F_2 = P \times A_2 = \left( \frac{F_1}{A_1} \right) \times A_2\)
4. Rearranging: $$ \frac{F_2}{F_1} = \frac{A_2}{A_1} $$
Since \(A_2\) is much larger than \(A_1\), the output force \(F_2\) is much larger than the input force \(F_1\). The machine acts as a force multiplier.

4. Atmospheric Pressure and The Barometer

Have you ever wondered how a rubber suction cup sticks so firmly to a smooth bathroom tile? When you press it flat, you squeeze the air out from underneath it. The invisible blanket of air surrounding the Earth is incredibly heavy and pushes down on everything. Because there is no air underneath the cup to push back, the heavy air outside presses it tightly against the wall.

Atmospheric Pressure: The pressure exerted by the weight of the Earth's atmosphere on any surface exposed to it. Standard atmospheric pressure at sea level is approximately \(101,325\text{ Pa}\) (or about \(1\text{ atm}\)).
Measurement of Atmospheric Pressure
Atmospheric pressure is measured using an instrument called a Barometer. The simplest form is the Simple Mercury Barometer, invented by Torricelli.

• It consists of a glass tube (about 1 meter long) filled completely with mercury and inverted into a bowl of mercury.
• The mercury column falls until the pressure exerted by its weight exactly balances the atmospheric pressure pushing down on the mercury in the bowl.
• At sea level, this column height is exactly 76 cm (or 760 mm) of mercury (Hg).
• Hence, standard atmospheric pressure is often expressed as \(76\text{ cm of Hg}\). Using \(P = h\rho g\) for mercury, we arrive at the value of roughly \(10^5\text{ Pa}\).

5. Worked Problems

Problem 6.1: Calculating Solid Pressure [3 Marks]
A box of weight \(600\text{ N}\) rests on a floor on a surface area of \(0.5\text{ m}^2\). Calculate the pressure exerted. If stood on one leg of area \(0.02\text{ m}^2\), what is the new pressure?
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. Initial Case (Lying Flat):
    Force (Thrust) \(F = 600\text{ N}\)
    Area \(A_1 = 0.5\text{ m}^2\)
    $$P_1 = \frac{F}{A_1} = \frac{600\text{ N}}{0.5\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{1200\text{ Pa}}$$ [1.5 Marks]

2. Second Case (Standing on one leg):
    Force remains unchanged, \(F = 600\text{ N}\)
    New Area \(A_2 = 0.02\text{ m}^2\)
    $$P_2 = \frac{F}{A_2} = \frac{600\text{ N}}{0.02\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{30,000\text{ Pa}}$$ [1.5 Marks]
Problem 6.2: The Hydraulic Press [3 Marks]
A hydraulic press has a small piston of area \(20\text{ cm}^2\) and a large piston of area \(200\text{ cm}^2\). A force of \(100\text{ N}\) is applied on the small piston. Calculate the force produced at the large piston.
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. Identify given variables:
    Small piston area, \(A_1 = 20\text{ cm}^2\)
    Large piston area, \(A_2 = 200\text{ cm}^2\)
    Force on small piston, \(F_1 = 100\text{ N}\)
    (Note: We do not need to convert areas to \(\text{m}^2\) because their ratio is dimensionless, provided units are consistent.) [1 Mark]

2. Apply Pascal's Principle Equation:
    $$\frac{F_2}{A_2} = \frac{F_1}{A_1} \implies F_2 = F_1 \times \frac{A_2}{A_1}$$ [1 Mark]

3. Substitute and Calculate:
    $$F_2 = 100 \times \frac{200}{20} = 100 \times 10 = \mathbf{1000\text{ N}}$$ [1 Mark]

6. Give Scientific Reasons

Q: Why do school bags have wide shoulder straps?
Reason: Wide straps increase the surface area of contact over the student's shoulder. Since pressure is inversely proportional to area (\(P = F/A\)), the larger area reduces the pressure exerted by the heavy bag, preventing pain and making it more comfortable to carry.
Q: Why are dams constructed with thicker walls at the bottom?
Reason: According to the law of hydrostatic pressure (\(P = h\rho g\)), the pressure exerted by water increases with depth. The bottom of the dam experiences the maximum lateral water pressure, so the walls must be built thicker at the base to withstand this enormous force without collapsing.
Q: Why do astronauts wear special pressurized space suits?
Reason: The human body maintains internal blood pressure that perfectly balances the atmospheric pressure on Earth. In space, there is no atmospheric pressure (vacuum). Without a pressurized suit, the internal blood pressure would cause blood vessels to burst, and body fluids would start to boil.
