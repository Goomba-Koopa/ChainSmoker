Chemistry • Topic 04

Atoms, Molecules, Atomicity & Radicals

Atom vs molecule distinction, atomicity classifications (monoatomic He/Ar, diatomic H₂/O₂/N₂/Cl₂, polyatomic O₃/P₄/S₈), electropositive radicals (+1 to +4) and electronegative radicals (−1 to −4), and the criss-cross valency method for formula writing.

1. Atom vs. Molecule Distinction

Imagine taking a pure copper wire and cutting it in half repeatedly. Eventually, you would reach a particle so incredibly small that it can no longer be cut while still remaining copper. This fundamental building block is an atom. However, many atoms are too unstable to exist alone in nature, so they bind together into small groups, acting as a single unit—much like how individual people form a family unit. This stable group of bonded atoms is called a molecule.

Atom: The smallest particle of an element that can take part in a chemical reaction. It may or may not be capable of independent existence.

Molecule: The smallest particle of an element or a compound that is capable of independent existence and retains all the chemical properties of that substance.
Characteristic Atom Molecule
Independent Existence Usually cannot exist independently (except noble gases). Always capable of independent, stable existence.
Chemical Reactivity Highly reactive (due to unstable electron configurations). Generally less reactive and more stable than individual atoms.
Subdivision Cannot be subdivided into simpler substances by ordinary chemical means. Can be subdivided into individual constituent atoms through chemical reactions.
Composition Composed of subatomic particles (protons, neutrons, electrons). Composed of two or more chemically bonded atoms.

2. Atomicity Classifications

When you fill a birthday balloon with helium gas, you are filling it with individual, separated helium atoms bouncing around independently. But when you breathe in oxygen from the air, you are inhaling paired molecules—two oxygen atoms permanently holding hands. The number of atoms that combine to form a single, independent molecule of an element dictates its structure and classification. We term this specific number "atomicity."

Atomicity: The total number of atoms present in one single molecule of an element. Elements are classified based on their atomicity as monoatomic, diatomic, triatomic, or polyatomic.
Standard Atomicity Classifications
1. Monoatomic (Atomicity = 1):
Molecules that consist of only a single atom. These are highly stable elements that do not need to bond with others.
Examples: Helium (He), Neon (Ne), Argon (Ar), and most solid metals like Sodium (Na) or Iron (Fe).

2. Diatomic (Atomicity = 2):
Molecules consisting of exactly two chemically bonded atoms of the same element.
Examples: Hydrogen (\(\text{H}_2\)), Oxygen (\(\text{O}_2\)), Nitrogen (\(\text{N}_2\)), and Chlorine (\(\text{Cl}_2\)).

3. Triatomic (Atomicity = 3):
Molecules consisting of exactly three atoms of the same element.
Example: Ozone (\(\text{O}_3\)).

4. Polyatomic (Atomicity = 4 or more):
Molecules containing more than three atoms of the same element bonded together in complex structures like rings or tetrahedrons.
Examples: Phosphorus (\(\text{P}_4\)) is tetratomic. Sulphur (\(\text{S}_8\)) is octatomic.

3. Electropositive & Electronegative Radicals

When you dissolve ordinary table salt in a bowl of warm soup, the solid crystals break apart into invisible charged particles that drift through the water. These charged particles are crucial for conducting electricity in the human body. In chemistry, any atom or group of atoms that carries an electrical charge is called a radical or an ion. They behave as a single unit during chemical reactions, snapping together like biological magnets.

Radical (Ion): An atom or a group of bonded atoms that behaves as a single chemical unit and possesses a net positive or negative electrical charge.

Valency: The combining capacity of an element or a radical. It is numerically equal to the magnitude of the charge on the radical.
Type Definition Common Examples & Valencies
Electropositive Radicals (Cations) Radicals carrying a net positive charge (usually formed by losing electrons). They are often metallic. • Monovalent (+1): Sodium (\(\text{Na}^+\)), Potassium (\(\text{K}^+\)), Ammonium (\(\text{NH}_4^+\))
• Divalent (+2): Calcium (\(\text{Ca}^{2+}\)), Magnesium (\(\text{Mg}^{2+}\)), Zinc (\(\text{Zn}^{2+}\))
• Trivalent (+3): Aluminium (\(\text{Al}^{3+}\)), Iron(III) (\(\text{Fe}^{3+}\))
• Tetravalent (+4): Lead(IV) (\(\text{Pb}^{4+}\))
Electronegative Radicals (Anions) Radicals carrying a net negative charge (usually formed by gaining electrons). They are often non-metallic. • Monovalent (−1): Chloride (\(\text{Cl}^-\)), Hydroxide (\(\text{OH}^-\)), Nitrate (\(\text{NO}_3^-\))
• Divalent (−2): Oxide (\(\text{O}^{2-}\)), Sulphide (\(\text{S}^{2-}\)), Sulphate (\(\text{SO}_4^{2-}\)), Carbonate (\(\text{CO}_3^{2-}\))
• Trivalent (−3): Nitride (\(\text{N}^{3-}\)), Phosphate (\(\text{PO}_4^{3-}\))
• Tetravalent (−4): Carbide (\(\text{C}^{4-}\))

4. The Criss-Cross Valency Method

Imagine you are trying to attach interlocking puzzle pieces. One piece has three outward pegs (valency of 3), while the other has only two inward slots (valency of 2). To make a perfectly smooth, closed shape with no loose ends, you would need exactly two of the first piece and three of the second piece. The criss-cross method uses this exact mathematical logic to instantly determine the chemical formula of any compound by balancing the positive and negative valencies.

Rules for the Criss-Cross Method
1. Write the symbols: Write the symbol of the electropositive radical (metal or ammonium) on the left, and the electronegative radical (non-metal or polyatomic ion) on the right.

2. Write the valencies: Write the respective valency numbers at the top right corner of each symbol (ignoring the + or - signs).

3. Simplify if possible: If the valency numbers share a common numerical factor, divide them by that factor to get a simple whole-number ratio.

4. Criss-cross: Swap the valency numbers, bringing them down to the lower right corner (subscript) of the opposite radical. If the subscript is 1, it is omitted. Polyatomic radicals must be enclosed in brackets if their subscript is greater than 1.

5. Worked Problems

Problem 1.1: Criss-Cross Formula Writing [3 Marks]
Using the criss-cross method, derive the formula for: (i) Aluminium Oxide, (ii) Iron(III) Sulphate, (iii) Calcium Phosphate. Show all steps clearly.
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. (i) Aluminium Oxide:
    • Symbols & Valencies: \(\text{Al}^{3}\) and \(\text{O}^{2}\)
    • Criss-cross: The 3 goes to O, the 2 goes to Al.
    • Final Formula: \(\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3\) [1 Mark]

2. (ii) Iron(III) Sulphate:
    • Symbols & Valencies: \(\text{Fe}^{3}\) and \(\text{SO}_4^{2}\)
    • Criss-cross: The 3 goes to the entire \(\text{SO}_4\) group (use brackets), the 2 goes to Fe.
    • Final Formula: \(\text{Fe}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3\) [1 Mark]

3. (iii) Calcium Phosphate:
    • Symbols & Valencies: \(\text{Ca}^{2}\) and \(\text{PO}_4^{3}\)
    • Criss-cross: The 2 goes to the \(\text{PO}_4\) group, the 3 goes to Ca.
    • Final Formula: \(\text{Ca}_3(\text{PO}_4)_2\) [1 Mark]
Problem 1.2: Atomicity Classification [3 Marks]
Classify the following as monoatomic, diatomic, or polyatomic: Neon, Chlorine gas, Ozone, Phosphorus, Hydrogen. State the atomicity number for each.
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

• Neon (Ne): Monoatomic, Atomicity = 1. [0.5 Marks]
• Chlorine gas (\(\text{Cl}_2\)): Diatomic, Atomicity = 2. [0.5 Marks]
• Ozone (\(\text{O}_3\)): Polyatomic (specifically Triatomic), Atomicity = 3. [1 Mark]
• Phosphorus (\(\text{P}_4\)): Polyatomic (specifically Tetratomic), Atomicity = 4. [0.5 Marks]
• Hydrogen (\(\text{H}_2\)): Diatomic, Atomicity = 2. [0.5 Marks]

6. Give Scientific Reasons

Reasoning 2.1: Atom vs Molecule Stability
Why do noble gases exist as free atoms, whereas elements like oxygen and hydrogen strictly exist as molecules in nature?
Model Examiner Answer:
Noble gases (like Helium or Neon) have a completely filled outermost electron shell, making them highly stable and chemically unreactive; thus, they are capable of independent existence as monoatomic structures. In contrast, oxygen and hydrogen have incomplete outermost electron shells. They must chemically combine (bond) with other atoms to achieve a stable electron configuration, forcing them to exist as diatomic molecules rather than isolated atoms.
Reasoning 2.2: Criss-Cross Simplification
Why is the formula for Magnesium Oxide written as \(\text{MgO}\) and not \(\text{Mg}_2\text{O}_2\)?
Model Examiner Answer:
The valency of Magnesium (\(\text{Mg}\)) is +2 and the valency of Oxygen (\(\text{O}\)) is -2. According to the rules of the criss-cross method, if the valencies share a common numerical factor (in this case, 2), they must be simplified by dividing by that factor to obtain the simplest whole-number ratio. Therefore, the 2:2 ratio simplifies to 1:1, resulting in the correct empirical formula \(\text{MgO}\).
