Light: Reflection, Mirrors & Shadows

Rectilinear propagation of light, formation of shadows and eclipses, laws of reflection (∠i = ∠r), lateral inversion, and the inclined-mirror formula (n = 360°/θ − 1).

1. Rectilinear Propagation of Light

If you have ever shined a laser pointer across a dusty room or observed sunlight streaming through a small gap in thick curtains, you might have noticed that the beam of light travels in a perfectly straight line without bending or spinning. In physics, we analyze this straight-line nature of light by comparing it to physical motion systems. Unlike a spinning top which demonstrates rotatory motion, a light ray in a uniform medium exclusively demonstrates pure forward translatory motion.

Rectilinear Propagation of Light: The fundamental property of light which states that in a homogeneous and transparent medium, light always travels in a straight line.
Feature Translatory Motion System Rotatory Motion System
Motion Path Object moves along a straight or curved path where all particles move the same distance. Object spins or revolves around a fixed central axis.
Application to Light A ray of light traveling through empty space perfectly models pure translatory (linear) motion. Light rays do not spin around their own axis as they propagate; thus, they lack rotatory motion.
Energy Transfer Energy is carried forward continuously from the source to the destination. Energy is localized to the rotating object unless translated forward.
Observation Pin-hole camera images and shadow formation provide direct evidence. Spinning gears, wheels, and planetary rotation.

2. Formation of Shadows & Eclipses

When you make hand puppets in front of a bright projector, the wall behind your hands goes dark. Because light travels in straight lines (rectilinear propagation), it cannot bend around an opaque obstacle. The region where light is blocked creates a dark patch known as a shadow. On a cosmic scale, shadows cast by celestial bodies like the Earth and Moon result in the spectacular phenomena we call eclipses.

Shadows & Eclipses: A shadow is the dark space formed behind an opaque object when it blocks the path of light from a source. A shadow has two parts: the umbra (region of total darkness) and the penumbra (region of partial darkness). An eclipse occurs when celestial bodies cast shadows on one another.
Characteristic Solar Eclipse Lunar Eclipse
Alignment Sun — Moon — Earth. The Moon comes precisely between the Sun and the Earth. Sun — Earth — Moon. The Earth comes precisely between the Sun and the Moon.
Shadow Cast By The Moon casts its shadow onto the surface of the Earth. The Earth casts its shadow onto the surface of the Moon.
Occurrence Time Can only occur during a New Moon day. Can only occur during a Full Moon night.
Visibility & Duration Visible from a very small region on Earth; lasts for a few minutes. Visible from the entire night-side of Earth; can last for hours.

3. The Laws of Reflection

Imagine bouncing a tennis ball against a smooth brick wall at a specific angle; you will intuitively know that the ball will rebound at the exact same angle on the opposite side. Light behaves similarly when it strikes a smooth, polished surface like a mirror. The incoming ray bounces back into the same medium, following strict geometric rules.

Laws of Reflection: 1. The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal to the mirror surface at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
2. The angle of incidence (\(\angle i\)) is always exactly equal to the angle of reflection (\(\angle r\)). Mathematically, \(\angle i = \angle r\).

4. Lateral Inversion in Plane Mirrors

If you raise your right hand in front of a bathroom mirror, your reflection appears to raise its left hand. Similarly, the word 'AMBULANCE' is deliberately painted reversed on emergency vehicles so that when a driver looks in their rear-view mirror, the word reads perfectly normal. This optical side-to-side reversal is a direct consequence of reflection.

Lateral Inversion: The phenomenon where the right side of an object appears as the left side of its image in a plane mirror, and vice versa. This occurs because the mirror reverses the front-to-back direction of light rays, causing a left-right perceptual flip.
Property Real Object Plane Mirror Image
Orientation Standard left-right orientation. Laterally inverted (left and right are swapped).
Nature of Image Physical matter emitting or reflecting light. Virtual (cannot be caught on a screen) and Erect (upright).
Size Comparison Has physical dimensions. Exactly the same size as the real object.
Distance At distance \(d\) in front of the mirror. Appears at distance \(d\) behind the mirror.

5. Images in Inclined Mirrors

When you stand in a clothing store dressing room that has two mirrors joined at a corner, you will see multiple reflections of yourself. When two plane mirrors are kept inclined to each other at an angle, the image formed by the first mirror acts as a virtual object for the second mirror, leading to multiple images.

The Inclined-Mirror Formula
To find the number of images (\(n\)) formed by two plane mirrors inclined at an angle \(\theta\):

1. Calculate the ratio: $$m = \frac{360^\circ}{\theta}$$
2. If \(m\) is an even integer:
    The number of images is \(n = m - 1\). This formula applies for all positions of the object.

3. If \(m\) is an odd integer:
    • If the object is placed symmetrically (on the angle bisector): \(n = m - 1\).
    • If the object is placed asymmetrically: \(n = m\).

Note: For parallel mirrors (\(\theta = 0^\circ\)), \(n = \frac{360^\circ}{0^\circ}\), which approaches infinity (\(\infty\)), meaning an infinite number of images are formed.

6. Worked Problems

Problem 3.1: Multiple Images in Inclined Mirrors [3 Marks]
Two plane mirrors are inclined at an angle of 60° to each other. An object is placed asymmetrically between them. How many images are formed by this arrangement? Show full working.
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. Identify the given angle:
    \(\theta = 60^\circ\) [1 Mark]

2. Calculate the ratio \(m\):
    $$m = \frac{360^\circ}{\theta} = \frac{360^\circ}{60^\circ} = 6$$ [1 Mark]

3. Apply the formula for an even integer:
    Since \(m = 6\) is an even integer, the formula for the number of images \(n\) is:
    \(n = \frac{360^\circ}{\theta} - 1\)
    \(n = 6 - 1 = \mathbf{5\text{ images}}\) [1 Mark]
Problem 3.2: Calculating the Angle of Reflection [2 Marks]
A light ray strikes a plane mirror such that the angle between the incident ray and the mirror surface is 35°. Find the angle of incidence and the angle of reflection.
Step-by-Step Examiner Solution:

1. Calculate Angle of Incidence (\(\angle i\)):
    The normal is perpendicular (90°) to the mirror surface.
    Therefore, \(\angle i = 90^\circ - \text{angle with surface}\)
    \(\angle i = 90^\circ - 35^\circ = \mathbf{55^\circ}\) [1 Mark]

2. Calculate Angle of Reflection (\(\angle r\)):
    According to the First Law of Reflection, \(\angle i = \angle r\).
    Therefore, \(\angle r = \mathbf{55^\circ}\) [1 Mark]

7. Give Scientific Reasons

Reasoning Question 1
Why does a solar eclipse not occur on every new moon day?
Model Answer:
A solar eclipse does not occur on every new moon day because the Moon's orbit is tilted by about 5° relative to Earth's orbit around the Sun. Due to this tilt, the shadows cast by the Moon usually fall above or below the Earth. A solar eclipse only occurs when the Sun, Moon, and Earth align perfectly on the same geometrical plane.
Reasoning Question 2
Why do letters printed on a piece of paper appear normal, but when viewed through a plane mirror they are unreadable?
Model Answer:
When text is viewed in a plane mirror, it undergoes lateral inversion, where the left side of the letters becomes the right side and vice-versa. This optical side-to-side reversal causes the asymmetrical letters to flip backwards, making standard printed text appear unreadable.
